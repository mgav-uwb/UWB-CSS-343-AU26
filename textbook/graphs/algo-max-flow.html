Maximum Flow & Minimum Cut

Data Structures & Algorithms. Algorithm.
How much material can be pushed from a source to a sink through a network of capacitated pipes, and what bottleneck stops it. Flow networks, capacity and conservation, \(s\)-\(t\) cuts and the weak-duality bound, residual graphs with their undo arcs, the Ford-Fulkerson augmenting-path method unrolled iteration by iteration (including one augmentation that pushes flow back along an edge already carrying flow), termination and integrality with integer capacities, the max-flow min-cut theorem with proof, the Edmonds-Karp breadth-first refinement and its \(O(VE^2)\) bound, the pathological-capacity example that motivates it, bipartite matching as a worked reduction, and exercises with solutions.

A pipeline network carries oil from a well to a refinery; every pipe has a throughput limit, and junctions neither store nor create oil. A road network moves cars from a stadium to a highway; every street has a lane capacity. A scheduling system assigns applicants to jobs; every applicant takes one job and every job takes one applicant. All three ask the same question: given a directed graph whose edges have capacities, how much can be sent from a designated source \(s\) to a designated sink \(t\) at once? The answer is the maximum flow. It comes with a companion answer that certifies it: some set of edges, a cut, whose total capacity equals that maximum and which every unit of flow must cross. Maximum flow and minimum cut are two descriptions of one number, and the algorithm that computes either computes both.

1. Flow networks: capacity and conservation

Definition (flow network) A flow network is a directed graph \(G = (V, E)\) with a nonnegative integer capacity \(c(u,v) \ge 0\) on each edge \(u \to v\), together with two distinguished vertices, the source \(s\) and the sink \(t \ne s\). Edges not present are given capacity \(0\). The representation is the weighted adjacency list (or matrix) of the graph-representations handout, with the weight read as a capacity.
Definition (flow) A flow is a function \(f\) assigning a number \(f(u,v)\) to every edge, subject to two constraints:
  • Capacity: \(0 \le f(u,v) \le c(u,v)\) for every edge. No pipe carries more than it can hold, and nothing flows backwards along an edge.
  • Conservation: for every vertex \(v\) other than \(s\) and \(t\), \[ \sum_{u \,:\, u \to v} f(u,v) \;=\; \sum_{w \,:\, v \to w} f(v,w). \] What arrives leaves. Interior vertices are junctions, not tanks.
The value of the flow is the net amount leaving the source, \[ |f| \;=\; \sum_{w \,:\, s \to w} f(s,w) \;-\; \sum_{u \,:\, u \to s} f(u,s), \] and the maximum-flow problem is to find a flow of largest value.

Two conventions keep the statement clean. Capacities are integers throughout this handout; §6 explains exactly what that buys and what breaks without it. And the network is assumed to have no edge into \(s\) and no edge out of \(t\); this costs nothing (such edges can carry flow only in circulation, never in a route from \(s\) to \(t\)) and lets \(|f|\) be read simply as \(\sum_w f(s,w)\).

The running network

One network anchors every trace below: four vertices \(s, a, b, t\) and five edges.

A first guess: send \(9\) down \(s \to a\) and \(7\) down \(s \to b\), for \(16\). Conservation refuses it. Vertex \(a\) would have \(9\) arriving and at most \(6 + 4 = 10\) of outgoing capacity, which is fine, but \(b\) would then have \(7 + 4 = 11\) arriving and only \(8\) of outgoing capacity. Something has to give, and the next section says exactly how much.

2. Cuts, and the bound they give

Definition (\(s\)-\(t\) cut) An \(s\)-\(t\) cut is a partition of \(V\) into two sets \((S, T)\) with \(s \in S\), \(t \in T\), and \(T = V \setminus S\). Its capacity is the total capacity of the edges pointing from \(S\) into \(T\): \[ \operatorname{cap}(S,T) \;=\; \sum_{u \in S} \sum_{v \in T} c(u,v). \] Edges pointing from \(T\) back into \(S\) contribute nothing to the capacity. A minimum cut is one of least capacity.

Since a cut is determined by its \(S\) side, and \(S\) must contain \(s\) and exclude \(t\), a network with \(V\) vertices has exactly \(2^{V-2}\) cuts. The running network has \(2^2 = 4\), and they can simply be listed:

cut \((S,T)\) edges \(S \to T\) capacity
\(S=\{s\}\) \(s\to a\,(9)\), \(s\to b\,(7)\)16
\(S=\{s,a\}\) \(s\to b\,(7)\), \(a\to b\,(4)\), \(a\to t\,(6)\)17
\(S=\{s,b\}\) \(s\to a\,(9)\), \(b\to t\,(8)\)17
\(S=\{s,a,b\}\) \(a\to t\,(6)\), \(b\to t\,(8)\)14

Note the third row: the edge \(a \to b\) points from \(T\) back into \(S\), so it is not counted, and the cut \(\{s,b\}\) costs \(9 + 8 = 17\), not \(21\). The minimum over all four cuts is \(14\), at \(S = \{s,a,b\}\). The next two results say that \(14\) is not merely a listing curiosity: it is an upper bound on every flow, and it is attained.

Lemma (flow across a cut) For any flow \(f\) and any \(s\)-\(t\) cut \((S,T)\), \[ |f| \;=\; \underbrace{\sum_{u \in S}\sum_{v \in T} f(u,v)}_{\text{flow leaving } S} \;-\; \underbrace{\sum_{v \in T}\sum_{u \in S} f(v,u)}_{\text{flow returning to } S}. \] Every cut measures the same net flow, no matter where it is drawn.
Proof. For a vertex \(v\), write \(\operatorname{net}(v) = \sum_{w} f(v,w) - \sum_{u} f(u,v)\), the net outflow at \(v\). Conservation says \(\operatorname{net}(v) = 0\) for every \(v \notin \{s,t\}\), and the definition of value says \(\operatorname{net}(s) = |f|\). Sum over all of \(S\): \[ \sum_{v \in S} \operatorname{net}(v) \;=\; |f| + 0 + \cdots + 0 \;=\; |f|. \] Now expand the left side edge by edge. An edge with both endpoints inside \(S\) contributes \(+f\) at its tail and \(-f\) at its head, cancelling. An edge from \(S\) to \(T\) contributes only \(+f\), at its tail. An edge from \(T\) to \(S\) contributes only \(-f\), at its head. Edges with both endpoints in \(T\) contribute nothing. What survives is exactly the stated difference. ∎
Theorem (weak duality) For any flow \(f\) and any \(s\)-\(t\) cut \((S,T)\), \(\;|f| \le \operatorname{cap}(S,T)\). Consequently \(\max_f |f| \le \min_{(S,T)} \operatorname{cap}(S,T)\).
Proof. Start from the lemma. The returning term is a sum of flows, hence \(\ge 0\), so dropping it can only raise the right side: \(|f| \le \sum_{u \in S, v \in T} f(u,v)\). Each term obeys \(f(u,v) \le c(u,v)\) by the capacity constraint, so the sum is at most \(\sum_{u \in S, v \in T} c(u,v) = \operatorname{cap}(S,T)\). Taking the maximum on the left and the minimum on the right preserves the inequality. ∎
Example (weak duality, checked numerically) Take the flow \(f\) on the running network with \(f(s,a)=9\), \(f(s,b)=4\), \(f(a,b)=4\), \(f(a,t)=5\), \(f(b,t)=8\). Capacity holds edge by edge (\(9\le9\), \(4\le7\), \(4\le4\), \(5\le6\), \(8\le8\)) and conservation holds at both interior vertices: at \(a\), \(9\) in and \(4+5 = 9\) out; at \(b\), \(4+4 = 8\) in and \(8\) out. Its value is \(|f| = 9+4 = 13\). The lemma says every cut reports \(13\):
cutflow \(S \to T\)flow \(T \to S\)netcapacity
\(\{s\}\)\(9+4=13\)01316
\(\{s,a\}\)\(4+4+5=13\)01317
\(\{s,b\}\)\(9+8=17\)\(4\)1317
\(\{s,a,b\}\)\(5+8=13\)01314
The \(\{s,b\}\) row is the one that shows why the returning term matters: \(17\) units cross forward, but \(4\) of them come straight back along \(a \to b\), and the net is \(13\) like everywhere else. Weak duality is visible in the last two columns: \(13 \le 16, 17, 17, 14\). The tightest bound is \(14\), so no flow on this network can exceed \(14\), and this particular flow is at most \(1\) short of optimal. Whether it is short at all is the question §3 answers.

3. Residual graphs: slack and undo

Given a flow, how can it be improved? The naive move is to find a path from \(s\) to \(t\) all of whose edges still have spare capacity, and push more along it. That move alone is not enough: the flow of value \(13\) above has no such path (\(s \to a\) is saturated, and from \(s \to b\) the only continuation \(b \to t\) is saturated too), yet a flow of value \(14\) exists. The missing move is retraction: sending flow back along an edge that already carries some, freeing capacity downstream so it can be spent better. The residual graph packages both moves as ordinary edges, so that a single path search finds either.

Definition (residual graph) Given a flow \(f\) on \(G\), the residual graph \(G_f\) has the same vertices and, for every edge \(u \to v\) of \(G\), up to two arcs:
  • a forward arc \(u \to v\) of residual capacity \(c(u,v) - f(u,v)\), the unused slack, present when that is positive;
  • a backward arc \(v \to u\) of residual capacity \(f(u,v)\), the amount that could be undone, present when that is positive.
Write \(c_f(x,y)\) for the residual capacity of arc \(x \to y\) in \(G_f\). An augmenting path is any directed \(s \to t\) path in \(G_f\); its bottleneck is the minimum residual capacity along it.

A backward arc is not a physical pipe. It is the bookkeeping entry that says “up to \(f(u,v)\) units currently routed \(u \to v\) may be rerouted elsewhere.” The point of the construction is that pushing \(b\) units along an augmenting path, treating forward and backward arcs identically, always yields a valid flow of value \(|f| + b\).

Lemma (augmentation is legal) Let \(P\) be an augmenting path in \(G_f\) with bottleneck \(b > 0\). Define \(f'\) by adding \(b\) to \(f(u,v)\) for each forward arc \(u \to v\) of \(P\) and subtracting \(b\) from \(f(u,v)\) for each backward arc \(v \to u\) of \(P\). Then \(f'\) is a flow and \(|f'| = |f| + b\).
Proof. Capacity. On a forward arc, \(b \le c_f(u,v) = c(u,v) - f(u,v)\), so \(f'(u,v) = f(u,v) + b \le c(u,v)\), and it stays \(\ge 0\). On a backward arc, \(b \le c_f(v,u) = f(u,v)\), so \(f'(u,v) = f(u,v) - b \ge 0\), and it stays \(\le c(u,v)\).
Conservation. Let \(x\) be an interior vertex on \(P\); the path enters \(x\) by one arc and leaves by another (a path visits \(x\) once). Four cases, by the direction of each of the two arcs. If both are forward, \(x\) gains \(b\) of inflow and \(b\) of outflow. If both are backward, \(x\) loses \(b\) of outflow (the incoming arc undoes an edge \(x \to y\)) and loses \(b\) of inflow (the outgoing arc undoes an edge \(z \to x\)), again balanced. If the entering arc is forward and the leaving arc is backward, \(x\) gains \(b\) of inflow and loses \(b\) of inflow on another edge, so both sides are unchanged; symmetrically for the fourth case, where outflow rises on one edge and falls on another. Vertices not on \(P\) are untouched.
Value. \(P\) leaves \(s\) by exactly one arc, and it cannot be a backward arc, because a backward arc out of \(s\) would come from an edge into \(s\), and there are none. So exactly one term \(f(s,w)\) increases, by \(b\), giving \(|f'| = |f| + b\). ∎

4. Ford-Fulkerson

The augmentation lemma turns into an algorithm immediately: start with the zero flow, and while the residual graph has an \(s \to t\) path, push its bottleneck along it. This is the Ford-Fulkerson method, published by L. R. Ford and D. R. Fulkerson in 1956. It is a method, not a single algorithm, because it does not say which augmenting path to take, and the choice turns out to matter a great deal (§6, §8). 

  f = 0 on every edge
  while G_f has an s-to-t path P:
      b = min residual capacity along P     // the bottleneck
      push b along P                        // forward arcs +b, backward arcs -b
  return f

The implementation below keeps only the residual graph, in a matrix res initialized to the capacities. Pushing \(b\) along an arc \(u \to v\) does res[u][v] -= b; res[v][u] += b;, which simultaneously consumes forward slack and grows the undo arc, with no separate flow array to keep in step. The flow on an original edge is recovered afterwards as \(c(u,v) - \texttt{res}[u][v]\). The path search shown is a breadth-first search (BFS), which makes this the Edmonds-Karp version of §8; swapping the queue for a stack gives a depth-first search (DFS) and the generic method. 

// Ford-Fulkerson with BFS-chosen augmenting paths (Edmonds-Karp).
// res[u][v] is the residual capacity of u->v; initialize res to the capacities.
// On return, res holds the final residual graph and parent[] marks the min cut.
int maxFlow(vector<vector<int>>& res, int s, int t, vector<int>& parent) {
    int n = res.size(), value = 0;
    while (true) {
        parent.assign(n, -1);
        parent[s] = s;                            // s is its own parent: "seen"
        queue<int> q;  q.push(s);
        while (!q.empty() && parent[t] == -1) {   // BFS in the residual graph
            int u = q.front();  q.pop();
            for (int v = 0; v < n; v++)
                if (parent[v] == -1 && res[u][v] > 0) { parent[v] = u; q.push(v); }
        }
        if (parent[t] == -1) return value;        // no augmenting path: done

        int b = INT_MAX;                          // bottleneck along the path
        for (int v = t; v != s; v = parent[v]) b = min(b, res[parent[v]][v]);
        for (int v = t; v != s; v = parent[v]) {
            res[parent[v]][v] -= b;               // spend forward slack
            res[v][parent[v]] += b;               // grow the undo arc
        }
        value += b;
    }
}
// Ford-Fulkerson with BFS-chosen augmenting paths (Edmonds-Karp).
// res[u][v] is the residual capacity of u->v; initialize res to the capacities.
// On return, res holds the final residual graph and parent[] marks the min cut.
int maxFlow(int[][] res, int s, int t, int[] parent) {
    int n = res.length, value = 0;
    while (true) {
        Arrays.fill(parent, -1);
        parent[s] = s;                            // s is its own parent: "seen"
        Deque<Integer> q = new ArrayDeque<>();  q.add(s);
        while (!q.isEmpty() && parent[t] == -1) { // BFS in the residual graph
            int u = q.poll();
            for (int v = 0; v < n; v++)
                if (parent[v] == -1 && res[u][v] > 0) { parent[v] = u; q.add(v); }
        }
        if (parent[t] == -1) return value;        // no augmenting path: done

        int b = Integer.MAX_VALUE;                // bottleneck along the path
        for (int v = t; v != s; v = parent[v]) b = Math.min(b, res[parent[v]][v]);
        for (int v = t; v != s; v = parent[v]) {
            res[parent[v]][v] -= b;               // spend forward slack
            res[v][parent[v]] += b;               // grow the undo arc
        }
        value += b;
    }
}
# Ford-Fulkerson with BFS-chosen augmenting paths (Edmonds-Karp).
# res[u][v] is the residual capacity of u->v; initialize res to the capacities.
# Returns (value, parent); parent marks the min cut on the final iteration.
def max_flow(res, s, t):
    n, value = len(res), 0
    while True:
        parent = [-1] * n
        parent[s] = s                             # s is its own parent: "seen"
        q = deque([s])
        while q and parent[t] == -1:              # BFS in the residual graph
            u = q.popleft()
            for v in range(n):
                if parent[v] == -1 and res[u][v] > 0:
                    parent[v] = u
                    q.append(v)
        if parent[t] == -1:
            return value, parent                  # no augmenting path: done

        b, v = float("inf"), t                    # bottleneck along the path
        while v != s:
            b = min(b, res[parent[v]][v]); v = parent[v]
        v = t
        while v != s:
            res[parent[v]][v] -= b                # spend forward slack
            res[v][parent[v]] += b                # grow the undo arc
            v = parent[v]
        value += b
// Ford-Fulkerson with BFS-chosen augmenting paths (Edmonds-Karp).
// res[u][v] is the residual capacity of u->v; initialize res to the capacities.
// Returns (value, parent); parent marks the min cut on the final iteration.
fn max_flow(res: &mut Vec<Vec<i64>>, s: usize, t: usize) -> (i64, Vec<isize>) {
    let n = res.len();
    let mut value = 0i64;
    loop {
        let mut parent = vec![-1isize; n];
        parent[s] = s as isize;                   // s is its own parent: "seen"
        let mut q = VecDeque::from(vec![s]);
        while let Some(u) = q.pop_front() {       // BFS in the residual graph
            if parent[t] != -1 { break; }
            for v in 0..n {
                if parent[v] == -1 && res[u][v] > 0 { parent[v] = u as isize; q.push_back(v); }
            }
        }
        if parent[t] == -1 { return (value, parent); }   // no augmenting path

        let (mut b, mut v) = (i64::MAX, t);       // bottleneck along the path
        while v != s { let p = parent[v] as usize; b = b.min(res[p][v]); v = p; }
        v = t;
        while v != s {
            let p = parent[v] as usize;
            res[p][v] -= b;                       // spend forward slack
            res[v][p] += b;                       // grow the undo arc
            v = p;
        }
        value += b;
    }
}

Three notes on the representation. First, the matrix merges antiparallel edges: if the network has both \(u \to v\) of capacity \(3\) and \(v \to u\) of capacity \(5\), a single res pair cannot tell “\(3\) units of forward slack” from “\(3\) units of undo on the reverse edge.” The maximum value is unaffected, but if the per-edge flows are wanted, split one of the two edges by inserting a new vertex (exercise 6). Second, on sparse graphs replace the matrix by an adjacency list of arcs stored in pairs, each arc holding the index of its reverse arc, so BFS scans \(\Theta(E)\) instead of \(\Theta(V^2)\). Third, when the loop finally exits, the parent array is exactly the record of which vertices the last BFS could reach, and §7 proves that this set is the \(S\) side of a minimum cut: the algorithm hands back the certificate for free.

5. A full run, iteration by iteration

Ford-Fulkerson on the running network, with the augmenting paths chosen in an order a depth-first search would plausibly produce (it prefers \(a \to b\) over \(a \to t\) on the first descent). Each iteration shows the residual graph as four rows over the five original edges: the fixed capacity, the current flow, the forward slack \(c - f\), and the undo arc \(f\). Any arc with a positive entry in the “slack” row is usable in its printed direction; any arc with a positive entry in the “undo” row is usable in the reverse direction.

Three of the five forward arcs are gone: \(s \to a\), \(a \to b\) and \(b \to t\) are all saturated. Using only forward slack there is no way out of \(b\), so a search restricted to unsaturated original edges would stop here and report \(13\). The residual graph is not so restricted. Reading the shaded entries: \(s \to b\) has slack \(3\); the backward arc \(b \to a\) has capacity \(4\), because \(4\) units are currently routed \(a \to b\); and \(a \to t\) has slack \(1\). That is an \(s \to t\) path in \(G_f\): \[ s \;\xrightarrow{\ 3\ }\; b \;\xrightarrow{\ 4 \text{ (undo)}\ }\; a \;\xrightarrow{\ 1\ }\; t, \qquad \text{bottleneck } b = \min(3,4,1) = 1. \] Pushing \(1\) along it adds \(1\) to \(f(s,b)\), subtracts \(1\) from \(f(a,b)\), and adds \(1\) to \(f(a,t)\): \(f(s,b) = 5\), \(f(a,b) = 3\), \(f(a,t) = 6\), value \(14\). In physical terms: one unit that had been travelling \(s \to a \to b \to t\) is rerouted to travel \(s \to a \to t\), and the unit of capacity it vacated on \(b \to t\) is filled by a fresh unit sent \(s \to b \to t\). Two routes are rewritten to make room for a third. No sequence of pushes along unsaturated original edges can do this, which is precisely why the backward arcs exist.

Check that the result is still a legal flow. Capacity: \(9 \le 9\), \(5 \le 7\), \(3 \le 4\), \(6 \le 6\), \(8 \le 8\). Conservation at \(a\): \(9\) in, \(3 + 6 = 9\) out. At \(b\): \(5 + 3 = 8\) in, \(8\) out. Value: \(9 + 5 = 14\).

The search from \(s\) in this residual graph: \(s \to a\) has no slack, so the only step out of \(s\) is \(s \to b\) (slack \(2\)); from \(b\), the arc \(b \to t\) has no slack, but the undo arc \(b \to a\) (capacity \(3\)) is available, so \(a\) is reached; from \(a\), the forward arc \(a \to b\) (slack \(1\)) revisits a seen vertex, \(a \to t\) has no slack, and the undo arc \(a \to s\) returns to the source. The search exhausts itself having reached \(\{s, b, a\}\) and never \(t\). No augmenting path exists; the algorithm returns \(14\).

Take \(S = \{s,a,b\}\), the set the search reached, and \(T = \{t\}\). The edges crossing forward are \(a \to t\,(6)\) and \(b \to t\,(8)\), and both are saturated: \(f(a,t) = 6 = c(a,t)\) and \(f(b,t) = 8 = c(b,t)\). No edge crosses backward. So \(\operatorname{cap}(S,T) = 14 = |f|\), which by weak duality is only possible if the flow is maximum and the cut is minimum. The listing in §2 agrees: \(14\) was the smallest of the four cut capacities.

Two branches of the algorithm are on display beyond the ordinary ones. Iteration 1 has its bottleneck set by a middle edge rather than by the first or last edge of the path, so the push saturates neither the arc out of \(s\) nor the arc into \(t\). Iteration 4 uses a backward arc. Iteration 5 is the exit branch: a search that reaches some vertices but not \(t\). The remaining branch, a search from \(s\) that reaches nothing at all, occurs when every edge out of \(s\) is saturated at the moment the loop is entered again; that happens whenever the minimum cut is \(S = \{s\}\), which is the case in exercise 2.

6. Termination and integrality

Nothing so far guarantees the while loop ever stops. With integer capacities it does, for a blunt reason.

Theorem (termination and integrality) If every capacity is a nonnegative integer, Ford-Fulkerson halts, whatever rule it uses to pick augmenting paths, after at most \(C = \sum_{w} c(s,w)\) augmentations. Every intermediate flow, and the final one, is integral on every edge. Hence a maximum flow with integer values on every edge always exists.
Proof. Induct on the number of augmentations. The initial flow is \(0\), an integer on every edge. If \(f\) is integral then every residual capacity \(c(u,v)-f(u,v)\) and \(f(u,v)\) is an integer, so the bottleneck \(b\) is an integer, and \(b > 0\) forces \(b \ge 1\); adding or subtracting \(b\) keeps the flow integral. So every augmentation raises \(|f|\) by at least \(1\). Weak duality with the cut \(S = \{s\}\) gives \(|f| \le C\), so at most \(C\) augmentations can occur. The final flow is integral by the induction. ∎

The integrality half of that theorem is a workhorse. Whenever a combinatorial problem is encoded as a flow network with unit or integer capacities, the theorem promises that an optimal solution exists which makes whole-number decisions: an applicant is assigned to a job or not, never to two-thirds of one. §9 uses exactly this.

The cost bound that comes with it is weak. Finding one augmenting path by BFS or DFS costs \(\Theta(E)\) on an adjacency-list residual graph (or \(\Theta(V^2)\) on the matrix), and there may be \(C\) of them, so generic Ford-Fulkerson runs in \(O(EC)\). That is pseudo-polynomial: \(C\) is the numeric value of the capacities, not the size of the input. A network encoded in a few dozen bytes can have \(C\) in the billions, and the bound is achieved.

The pathological-capacity example Four vertices, five edges: \(s \to a\) and \(s \to b\) and \(a \to t\) and \(b \to t\), all of capacity \(M\), plus the middle edge \(a \to b\) of capacity \(1\). The maximum flow is \(2M\) (send \(M\) along \(s \to a \to t\) and \(M\) along \(s \to b \to t\); the cut \(S = \{s\}\) has capacity \(2M\)), and two augmentations suffice. But an adversarial path rule can alternate: push \(1\) along \(s \to a \to b \to t\), which saturates the middle edge, then push \(1\) along \(s \to b \to a \to t\), which uses the middle edge’s undo arc and restores its slack, then repeat. Each augmentation has bottleneck \(1\), so this takes \(2M\) iterations. Running it for \(M = 5\), \(M = 100\) and \(M = 10^6\) gives \(10\), \(200\) and \(2{,}000{,}000\) augmentations respectively, all for the same five-edge network. The cure is to stop letting the path rule be adversarial, which is §8.
Irrational capacities Drop integrality and termination can fail outright. With capacities involving \(\varphi = (\sqrt5 - 1)/2\), which satisfies \(\varphi^{\,k+2} = \varphi^{\,k} - \varphi^{\,k+1}\), there are networks and path choices on which the bottlenecks form a geometric sequence: the flow value increases forever, converging to a number that is not even the maximum flow. The bound “each augmentation gains at least \(1\)” is what fails, and nothing replaces it. This is a real limitation of the method as stated, not an artifact of floating point; it is another reason to fix the path rule rather than leave it free.

7. The max-flow min-cut theorem

Weak duality said \(\max |f| \le \min \operatorname{cap}\). The run in §5 ended with the two equal. That was not luck.

Theorem (max-flow min-cut, Ford-Fulkerson 1956) For a flow \(f\) in a network with source \(s\) and sink \(t\), the following three statements are equivalent:
  1. \(f\) is a maximum flow;
  2. the residual graph \(G_f\) contains no augmenting path;
  3. \(|f| = \operatorname{cap}(S,T)\) for some \(s\)-\(t\) cut \((S,T)\).
In particular the maximum flow value equals the minimum cut capacity, and Ford-Fulkerson, when it halts, returns both.
Proof. The three implications form a cycle.
(1) \(\Rightarrow\) (2). Contrapositive. If \(G_f\) has an augmenting path \(P\), its bottleneck \(b\) is positive (every arc of \(G_f\) has positive residual capacity by definition), and the augmentation lemma of §3 produces a legal flow of value \(|f| + b > |f|\). So \(f\) is not maximum.
(2) \(\Rightarrow\) (3). Suppose no augmenting path exists. Let \(S\) be the set of vertices reachable from \(s\) in \(G_f\), and \(T = V \setminus S\). Then \(s \in S\), and \(t \in T\) precisely because no \(s \to t\) path exists in \(G_f\), so \((S,T)\) is an \(s\)-\(t\) cut. Take any \(u \in S\) and \(v \in T\).
  • If \(u \to v\) is an edge of \(G\), it must be saturated: \(f(u,v) = c(u,v)\). Otherwise its residual slack \(c(u,v)-f(u,v)\) would be positive, putting the forward arc \(u \to v\) in \(G_f\), and \(v\) would be reachable from \(s\) through \(u\), contradicting \(v \in T\).
  • If \(v \to u\) is an edge of \(G\), it must carry no flow: \(f(v,u) = 0\). Otherwise the backward arc \(u \to v\) of residual capacity \(f(v,u) > 0\) would be in \(G_f\), again making \(v\) reachable.
Now apply the flow-across-a-cut lemma of §2: \[ |f| = \sum_{u \in S, v \in T} f(u,v) - \sum_{v \in T, u \in S} f(v,u) = \sum_{u \in S, v \in T} c(u,v) - 0 = \operatorname{cap}(S,T). \]
(3) \(\Rightarrow\) (1). Suppose \(|f| = \operatorname{cap}(S,T)\) for some cut. By weak duality every flow \(g\) satisfies \(|g| \le \operatorname{cap}(S,T) = |f|\), so \(f\) is maximum. The same inequality read the other way shows \((S,T)\) is a minimum cut: every cut \((S',T')\) satisfies \(\operatorname{cap}(S',T') \ge |f| = \operatorname{cap}(S,T)\). ∎

The step that carries the theorem is (2) \(\Rightarrow\) (3), and it is worth stating in words: when the search stops, the frontier it stopped at is the bottleneck. Every edge leaving the reached set is full, and every edge entering it is empty, so the reached set is a cut whose capacity the flow already meets. This is why the certificate is free: the last, failed path search computes it as a byproduct, in the parent array of the code in §4.

Trace the argument on iteration 5 of §5. The search reached \(S = \{s,a,b\}\). Edges from \(S\) to \(T = \{t\}\) are \(a \to t\) and \(b \to t\), and both are saturated, as the first bullet demands. There are no edges from \(T\) back to \(S\), so the second bullet is vacuous. The sum is \(6 + 8 = 14 = |f|\).

Two consequences worth naming The minimum cut need not be unique: exercise 2 has two of them. When several exist, the reachable set \(S\) from the theorem is the smallest one (every vertex in it is genuinely reachable); running the same argument backwards from \(t\) in the reverse residual graph produces the largest. Also, the theorem is a statement about capacities, not about the algorithm: it holds for any network, including those with irrational capacities on which Ford-Fulkerson may fail to halt.

8. Edmonds-Karp: choosing the path with BFS

The pathological example of §6 is a rule problem, not a network problem: the two-augmentation solution was there all along, and the adversary refused to take it by always routing through the capacity-\(1\) middle edge, which is the longest of the available \(s \to t\) paths. Jack Edmonds and Richard Karp’s fix (1972) is one line: always augment along a shortest augmenting path, measured in number of arcs, which means finding the path with a breadth-first search rather than a depth-first search. The BFS handout proves the property this relies on: BFS from \(s\) labels every reachable vertex with its exact hop distance from \(s\), so the path it recovers through the parent pointers is a minimum-arc path. The code in §4 is already written this way.

Theorem (Edmonds-Karp) With shortest augmenting paths, Ford-Fulkerson performs at most \(VE/2\) augmentations, so it runs in \(O(VE^2)\) time. The bound does not mention the capacities at all, and holds for real-valued capacities, so termination is guaranteed unconditionally.
Proof sketch. Let \(d_f(v)\) be the hop distance from \(s\) to \(v\) in \(G_f\). Two claims do the work.
Claim 1: distances never decrease. Augmenting along a shortest path only adds arcs that are reverses of arcs on that path, and each such reverse points from a vertex at BFS level \(i+1\) back to one at level \(i\). A new arc that goes backwards by one level cannot create a shorter route to anything: any path using it is at least as long as one that never did. So \(d_f(v)\) is nondecreasing over the run for every \(v\).
Claim 2: each edge is critical \(O(V)\) times. Call an arc \(u \to v\) critical in an augmentation if it set the bottleneck; it then disappears from the residual graph. It can only come back when some later augmentation pushes along \(v \to u\), and that later path is a shortest path, so at that moment \(d(u) = d(v) + 1\). At the earlier moment, \(d(v) = d(u) + 1\). Combining with Claim 1, the value of \(d(u)\) has grown by at least \(2\) between the two events. Since \(d(u) < V\) while \(u\) is reachable, each of the \(2E\) residual arcs can be critical at most \(V/2\) times, giving at most \(VE\) critical events; every augmentation has at least one critical arc, so there are at most \(VE\) augmentations. (A more careful count over the \(E\) original edges gives \(VE/2\).) Each augmentation costs one BFS, \(O(E)\), for \(O(VE^2)\). ∎

Faster methods exist and build on the same residual-graph idea: Dinic's algorithm augments along all shortest paths of a given length at once, for \(O(V^2E)\) in general and \(O(E\sqrt{V})\) on unit-capacity networks such as the matching networks of §9; capacity scaling prefers high-bottleneck paths for \(O(E^2 \log C)\); push-relabel methods abandon paths entirely. None of them changes the max-flow min-cut theorem or the meaning of the answer.

9. Bipartite matching, by reduction

The reason maximum flow is worth learning as a general tool is that many problems with no pipes in them are flow problems in disguise. The standard example:

Definition (bipartite graph, matching) A graph is bipartite if its vertices split into two sets \(X\) and \(Y\) such that every edge joins an \(X\) vertex to a \(Y\) vertex. A matching is a set of edges no two of which share a vertex; a maximum matching is one of largest size. In the assignment reading, \(X\) is applicants, \(Y\) is jobs, an edge means “this applicant is qualified for this job,” and a matching is a conflict-free set of hires.
The reduction From a bipartite graph \((X \cup Y, E)\) build a flow network:
  • add a source \(s\) and a sink \(t\);
  • an edge \(s \to x\) of capacity \(1\) for every \(x \in X\);
  • an edge \(x \to y\) of capacity \(1\) for every original edge \(\{x,y\}\), directed from \(X\) to \(Y\);
  • an edge \(y \to t\) of capacity \(1\) for every \(y \in Y\).
The network has \(|X|+|Y|+2\) vertices and \(|X|+|Y|+|E|\) edges, and is built in \(\Theta(V+E)\) time.
Proposition (the reduction is correct) The maximum flow value in that network equals the size of a maximum matching, and an integral maximum flow yields a maximum matching: take the edges \(x \to y\) with \(f(x,y) = 1\).
Proof. Matching \(\Rightarrow\) flow. Given a matching \(M\), put one unit on \(s \to x\), \(x \to y\), \(y \to t\) for each edge \(\{x,y\} \in M\). Capacities hold because \(M\) uses each \(x\) and each \(y\) at most once, so no unit-capacity edge is used twice; conservation holds at each \(x\) and \(y\) by construction. The value is \(|M|\).
Flow \(\Rightarrow\) matching. Take an integral maximum flow, which exists by the integrality theorem of §6. Every edge value is \(0\) or \(1\), since capacities are \(1\). Let \(M\) be the middle edges carrying \(1\). Each \(x\) has inflow at most \(1\) (the single edge \(s \to x\) has capacity \(1\)), so conservation gives outflow at most \(1\): \(x\) is in at most one edge of \(M\). Symmetrically each \(y\) has outflow at most \(1\) and so inflow at most \(1\). Hence \(M\) is a matching, and \(|M| = \) the flow into \(t = |f|\). The two directions give \(\max |f| = \max |M|\). ∎

Note where integrality is doing real work. Without it, the maximum flow might in principle split a unit into halves across two jobs, which is not an assignment; the theorem rules that out.

A worked instance

Three applicants \(x_1, x_2, x_3\), three jobs \(y_1, y_2, y_3\). Qualifications: \(x_1\) for \(y_1\) or \(y_2\); \(x_2\) for \(y_1\) only; \(x_3\) for \(y_2\) or \(y_3\).

Suppose a greedy pass over the applicants assigns \(x_1\) to \(y_1\) and then \(x_3\) to \(y_2\). Now \(x_2\), who is qualified only for \(y_1\), has nothing, and the matching has size \(2\). A greedy assignment is stuck there. The flow formulation is not, because the residual graph offers the undo arcs. In the network, the current flow is \(1\) on \(s \to x_1, x_1 \to y_1, y_1 \to t\) and on \(s \to x_3, x_3 \to y_2, y_2 \to t\). Search for an augmenting path: 

  s → x2       slack 1 (x2 unassigned)
    → y1       slack 1 (x2 is qualified for y1)
    → x1       undo arc: 1 unit is routed x1→y1, so it may be retracted
    → y2       slack 1 (x1 is also qualified for y2)
    → x3       undo arc: 1 unit is routed x3→y2
    → y3       slack 1 (x3 is also qualified for y3)
    → t        slack 1 (y3 is unfilled)
  bottleneck = 1

Pushing \(1\) unit flips every middle edge on the path: \(x_2 \to y_1\) goes to \(1\), \(x_1 \to y_1\) drops to \(0\), \(x_1 \to y_2\) goes to \(1\), \(x_3 \to y_2\) drops to \(0\), \(x_3 \to y_3\) goes to \(1\). The matching becomes \(\{x_2 y_1,\; x_1 y_2,\; x_3 y_3\}\) of size \(3\), and every applicant is placed. In matching language this is an alternating path from an unmatched \(x\) to an unmatched \(y\), alternating between non-matching and matching edges, and flipping it raises the matching size by one; the residual graph is what makes that classical notion a plain path search. Running Edmonds-Karp from the zero flow instead finds \(s\,x_1\,y_1\,t\), then \(s\,x_3\,y_2\,t\), then the seven-arc path above, and stops with value \(3\).

The minimum cut carries meaning too. Here the maximum flow saturates all three edges out of \(s\), so the reachable set is \(\{s\}\) and the minimum cut has capacity \(3\). In general, a minimum cut in the matching network corresponds to a smallest set of vertices touching every edge of the bipartite graph (a minimum vertex cover), and max-flow min-cut becomes the statement that in a bipartite graph the maximum matching size equals the minimum vertex cover size, which is König's theorem.

The same template covers a family of problems. Multiple sources or sinks: add a super-source with infinite-capacity edges to each real source. Vertex capacities: split \(v\) into \(v_{\text{in}} \to v_{\text{out}}\) with the vertex capacity on that edge, and rewire. Supplies and demands: capacities out of the super-source encode supplies, capacities into the super-sink encode demands, and a feasible schedule exists exactly when the maximum flow saturates the demand edges. Edge-disjoint paths: give every edge capacity \(1\), and the maximum flow value is the maximum number of edge-disjoint \(s \to t\) paths, with the minimum cut the fewest edges whose removal disconnects \(t\) from \(s\) (Menger's theorem).

10. Where this sits

Maximum flow is a graph algorithm built from the search primitives: the inner loop is one path search per augmentation, on a graph that changes between searches. That search is breadth-first search in the Edmonds-Karp form and depth-first search in the generic form, and the residual graph is stored with the adjacency structures of the graph-representations handout. The contrast with Dijkstra and Bellman-Ford is instructive: those also improve a candidate solution by local moves (relaxations), but their moves are monotone and never undone, whereas an augmentation may retract an earlier decision, which is exactly what lets a locally greedy start still reach a global optimum. It is also why maximum flow is not in the greedy paradigm: no exchange argument licenses committing to a first augmenting path, and the run in §5 shows a commitment being taken back. The \(O(VE^2)\) and \(O(EC)\) bounds are read in the notation of the asymptotic-notation handout, and the distinction they draw, a bound in the size of the input against a bound in the numeric value of the input, is the same pseudo-polynomial distinction that appears for the knapsack problem.

11. Exercises (with solutions)

  1. On the running network of §1, verify weak duality for the flow \(f(s,a)=4\), \(f(s,b)=7\), \(f(a,b)=1\), \(f(a,t)=3\), \(f(b,t)=8\): check it is a legal flow, give its value, and evaluate the flow-across-a-cut lemma on the cut \(S = \{s,b\}\).
    solution Capacity: \(4\le9\), \(7\le7\), \(1\le4\), \(3\le6\), \(8\le8\), all fine. Conservation at \(a\): in \(4\), out \(1+3=4\). At \(b\): in \(7+1 = 8\), out \(8\). Value \(|f| = 4+7 = 11\). Cut \(S=\{s,b\}\), \(T=\{a,t\}\): edges \(S \to T\) are \(s \to a\) carrying \(4\) and \(b \to t\) carrying \(8\), total \(12\); the edge \(T \to S\) is \(a \to b\) carrying \(1\). Net \(12 - 1 = 11 = |f|\), as the lemma requires. The capacity of that cut is \(9 + 8 = 17\), and \(11 \le 17\). The tightest bound is still the minimum cut, \(14\), so this flow is \(3\) short of optimal.
  2. Network on \(s,a,b,t\) with \(c(s,a)=3\), \(c(s,b)=2\), \(c(a,b)=2\), \(c(a,t)=2\), \(c(b,t)=3\). List all four cuts with their capacities, run Edmonds-Karp, and identify the minimum cut the algorithm reports. Is it unique?
    solution Cuts: \(S=\{s\}\): \(3+2 = 5\). \(S=\{s,a\}\): \(c(s,b)+c(a,b)+c(a,t) = 2+2+2 = 6\). \(S=\{s,b\}\): \(c(s,a)+c(b,t) = 3+3 = 6\). \(S=\{s,a,b\}\): \(c(a,t)+c(b,t) = 2+3 = 5\). Minimum \(5\), attained twice. Edmonds-Karp: BFS finds \(s\,a\,t\) (residual caps \(3,2\)), bottleneck \(2\); then \(s\,b\,t\) (caps \(2,3\)), bottleneck \(2\); then the three-arc \(s\,a\,b\,t\) (caps \(1,2,1\)), bottleneck \(1\); value \(5\). The next BFS: both \(s \to a\) (flow \(3\) of \(3\)) and \(s \to b\) (flow \(2\) of \(2\)) are saturated, so the search reaches nothing beyond \(s\) and the reported cut is \(S = \{s\}\), capacity \(5\). Not unique: \(S = \{s,a,b\}\) also has capacity \(5\). As §7 notes, the reachable set is the smallest minimum cut, which is why \(\{s\}\) is the one returned. This is also the branch where the final search reaches no new vertex at all.
  3. In iteration 4 of §5, suppose the backward arc \(b \to a\) were simply omitted from the residual graph (only unused capacity on original edges may be used). What value would Ford-Fulkerson report, and what goes wrong with the proof of the max-flow min-cut theorem?
    solution After iteration 3 the flow has value \(13\) and the unsaturated original edges are \(s \to b\) (slack \(3\)) and \(a \to t\) (slack \(1\)). There is no \(s \to t\) path among them: from \(s\) the only step is to \(b\), and \(b\)'s only out-edge \(b \to t\) is saturated. The crippled algorithm reports \(13\), one short of the maximum \(14\). The proof of (2) \(\Rightarrow\) (3) breaks at its second bullet. The reachable set would be \(S = \{s,b\}\), and the theorem needs every edge from \(T\) into \(S\) to carry no flow; but \(a \to b\) runs from \(T\) into \(S\) carrying \(4\). The flow-across-a-cut lemma then gives \(|f| = (9 + 8) - 4 = 13\) while \(\operatorname{cap}(\{s,b\}) = 17\), and no equality is obtained. The backward arc is exactly the mechanism that forces the second bullet to hold at termination.
  4. Give a network on which generic Ford-Fulkerson may take \(2{,}000{,}000\) augmentations while Edmonds-Karp takes \(2\), and state precisely the path sequence that produces the bad behavior.
    solution Vertices \(s,a,b,t\); \(c(s,a) = c(s,b) = c(a,t) = c(b,t) = 10^6\) and \(c(a,b) = 1\). Maximum flow \(2 \times 10^6\), certified by the cut \(S = \{s\}\) of capacity \(2 \times 10^6\). Bad sequence: alternate \(s \to a \to b \to t\) and \(s \to b \to a \to t\). The first uses the middle edge forward, bottleneck \(1\) (set by \(c(a,b)=1\)), and saturates it. The second uses the middle edge's undo arc, again with bottleneck \(1\), and restores its slack, so the pair of augmentations adds \(2\) to the value and returns the residual graph to a state differing only in the two big edges' slack. Repeating for \(10^6\) rounds gives \(2 \times 10^6\) augmentations. Edmonds-Karp never does this: the two-arc paths \(s \to a \to t\) and \(s \to b \to t\) are strictly shorter than any path through \(a \to b\), so BFS takes them first, each with bottleneck \(10^6\), and the third BFS finds no path. Two augmentations, whatever \(M\) is.
  5. Prove: if every edge capacity is multiplied by a positive constant \(k\), the maximum flow value is multiplied by \(k\) and the minimum cuts are unchanged as vertex sets. Does the number of Edmonds-Karp augmentations change?
    solution Scaling. If \(f\) is a flow for capacities \(c\), then \(kf\) is a flow for \(kc\) (capacity: \(0 \le kf \le kc\); conservation: both sides of every conservation equation scale by \(k\)), and its value is \(k|f|\); the same map with \(1/k\) inverts it, so the maximum flow scales exactly by \(k\). For cuts, \(\operatorname{cap}_{kc}(S,T) = \sum k\,c(u,v) = k \operatorname{cap}_c(S,T)\) for every cut, so the ordering of cuts by capacity is unchanged and the same vertex sets are minimum. Augmentations: no change if the algorithm is deterministic, because the BFS depends only on which residual capacities are positive, and positivity is preserved under multiplication by \(k > 0\); every bottleneck is simply \(k\) times what it was. This is another reading of why the \(O(VE^2)\) bound cannot mention capacities while the \(O(EC)\) bound must: scaling capacities by \(k\) changes \(C\) but changes nothing the algorithm does.
  6. The matrix representation of §4 cannot store both \(u \to v\) and \(v \to u\) with their own capacities. Show how to rewrite a network with such an antiparallel pair into one without, preserving the maximum flow value, and confirm the transformation on \(c(u,v) = 3\), \(c(v,u) = 5\).
    solution Insert a fresh vertex \(z\): delete the edge \(u \to v\) and add \(u \to z\) and \(z \to v\), both of capacity \(c(u,v)\). The edge \(v \to u\) is left alone, so no antiparallel pair remains. Any flow in the new network gives one in the old by setting \(f(u,v) = f(u,z) = f(z,v)\) (conservation at the degree-two vertex \(z\) forces those two to be equal), and any flow in the old gives one in the new the same way; values agree, so the maxima agree. On the example, \(u \to v\) of capacity \(3\) becomes \(u \to z\,(3)\) and \(z \to v\,(3)\), and \(v \to u\,(5)\) stays. The cost is one extra vertex and one extra edge per antiparallel pair, so \(O(E)\) of each in the worst case, which does not change the asymptotic bounds.
  7. Four applicants \(x_1, \dots, x_4\) and four jobs. Qualifications: \(x_1, x_2, x_3\) are each qualified only for \(y_1\) and \(y_2\), and \(x_4\) only for \(y_3\). Build the flow network, give the maximum matching, and exhibit a minimum cut of the network. Explain what the cut says about the instance.
    solution Network: \(s \to x_i\,(1)\) for \(i = 1,\dots,4\); the seven middle edges \(x_1y_1, x_1y_2, x_2y_1, x_2y_2, x_3y_1, x_3y_2, x_4y_3\), all of capacity \(1\); \(y_j \to t\,(1)\) for \(j = 1,\dots,4\). Maximum matching has size \(3\), for instance \(\{x_1y_1,\; x_2y_2,\; x_4y_3\}\); no larger is possible, since \(x_1, x_2, x_3\) compete for only two jobs, so at most two of them can be placed, plus \(x_4\). Edmonds-Karp from the zero flow finds \(s\,x_1\,y_1\,t\), \(s\,x_2\,y_2\,t\), \(s\,x_4\,y_3\,t\), each with bottleneck \(1\), and then a search that reaches \(S = \{s, x_1, x_2, x_3, y_1, y_2\}\) without reaching \(t\). That set is the minimum cut. Its crossing edges are \(s \to x_4\,(1)\), \(y_1 \to t\,(1)\) and \(y_2 \to t\,(1)\), for capacity \(3\): the six middle edges \(x_i \to y_j\) with \(i \le 3\) have both endpoints inside \(S\), and \(x_4 \to y_3\) has both endpoints outside. The cut names the obstruction. Its three edges correspond to the vertices \(y_1\), \(y_2\) and \(x_4\), a set of three vertices touching every qualification edge, that is, a minimum vertex cover. König's theorem in miniature: maximum matching \(3\) equals minimum vertex cover \(3\).
  8. The Edmonds-Karp analysis claims that when a critical arc \(u \to v\) reappears in the residual graph, the distance \(d(u)\) has increased by at least \(2\) since it was critical. Write out that step in full, and say where Claim 1 (distances never decrease) is used.
    solution Let the augmentation at time \(\tau_1\) have \(u \to v\) critical. That augmentation used a shortest path in \(G_{f_1}\) containing \(u \to v\), and along a shortest path every step advances the BFS level by exactly one, so \(d_{1}(v) = d_{1}(u) + 1\). Being critical, \(u \to v\) is removed from the residual graph, and the only way it can return is that some later augmentation, at time \(\tau_2 > \tau_1\), pushes flow along \(v \to u\), which restores slack on \(u \to v\). That augmentation also used a shortest path, this time containing \(v \to u\), so \(d_{2}(u) = d_{2}(v) + 1\). Chain the four facts: \[ d_{2}(u) = d_{2}(v) + 1 \;\ge\; d_{1}(v) + 1 = d_{1}(u) + 2, \] where the inequality \(d_{2}(v) \ge d_{1}(v)\) is exactly Claim 1, applied to \(v\) across the interval \([\tau_1, \tau_2]\). Without Claim 1 nothing connects the two times and the argument collapses. Since \(d(u)\) is a nonnegative integer less than \(V\) whenever \(u\) is reachable, and it gains \(2\) per reappearance, the arc can be critical at most \(V/2\) times.
