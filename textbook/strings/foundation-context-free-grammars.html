Context-Free Grammars & Parsing

John Backus described the syntax of ALGOL 58 with a grammar notation in 1959. Peter Naur used it in the ALGOL 60 report, and it became Backus–Naur form. J. W. Backus, ICIP Paris, 1959; P. Naur (ed.), Report on the Algorithmic Language ALGOL 60, 1960

Regular Expressions & Automata ends at a ceiling: a finite automaton has finitely many states, so it cannot count without bound, and no regular expression can describe balanced parentheses or \(\texttt{a}^n\texttt{b}^n\). Programming-language syntax, arithmetic expressions, JSON and HTML all live above that ceiling. This handout climbs one rung. A context-free grammar replaces the automaton’s fixed states with recursive rules, which gives it unbounded nesting for free; the machine that matches it replaces the automaton’s finite memory with a stack. Regular languages turn out to be exactly the context-free languages a stack is not needed for: a strict subset, and this handout proves both halves of that claim.

1. Grammars: four parts, one language

A regular expression describes a set of strings. A grammar generates one, by rewriting: start from a single symbol and repeatedly replace a symbol by one of its right-hand sides until only real characters remain. Everything you can produce that way is in the language; nothing else is.

Definition (context-free grammar) A context-free grammar (CFG) is a quadruple \(G = (V, \Sigma, R, S)\):
  • \(\Sigma\), the terminals: the alphabet the generated strings are written in (characters, or tokens such as +, id, while). Terminals are never rewritten.
  • \(V\), the nonterminals: the syntactic categories (Expression, Term), disjoint from \(\Sigma\). Each names a set of strings still to be determined.
  • \(R\), the productions (rules), each of the form \(A \to \alpha\) with \(A \in V\) a single nonterminal and \(\alpha \in (V \cup \Sigma)^{*}\) any string of terminals and nonterminals, possibly empty (written \(\varepsilon\)).
  • \(S \in V\), the start symbol.
“Context-free” is exactly the restriction that the left side of a production is one bare nonterminal: \(A\) may be replaced by \(\alpha\) wherever \(A\) occurs, with no regard for its neighbours. That single restriction is what makes efficient parsing possible.

The running grammar of this handout describes arithmetic expressions over a single kind of operand, written a (read it as “any identifier or number”: a real lexer would deliver one token for it). Terminals \(\Sigma = \{\texttt{a}, \texttt{+}, \texttt{*}, \texttt{(}, \texttt{)}\}\); one nonterminal \(V = \{E\}\); start symbol \(E\): 

  G1 (ambiguous):
      E → E + E
      E → E * E
      E → ( E )
      E → a

Alternatives for the same nonterminal are usually stacked with |: \(E \to E + E \mid E * E \mid (\,E\,) \mid \texttt{a}\). Note the self-reference: \(E\) appears on both sides. That recursion is the whole point, and it is what a finite automaton cannot imitate.

Definition (derives; the language of a grammar) Write \(\alpha \Rightarrow \beta\) (“\(\alpha\) derives \(\beta\) in one step”) when \(\beta\) is obtained from \(\alpha\) by replacing one occurrence of a nonterminal \(A\) by the right-hand side of some production \(A \to \gamma\). Write \(\alpha \Rightarrow^{*} \beta\) for zero or more such steps. A string of terminals and nonterminals reachable from \(S\) is a sentential form; one containing only terminals is a sentence. The language of \(G\) is \[ L(G) \;=\; \{\, w \in \Sigma^{*} \;:\; S \Rightarrow^{*} w \,\}. \] A language is context-free if it equals \(L(G)\) for some CFG \(G\).
Definition (parsing) Given \(G\) and a string \(w\), the recognition problem asks whether \(w \in L(G)\); the parsing problem asks for a derivation (equivalently, a parse tree) witnessing it. A compiler needs the second: the tree is the program’s structure, and the rest of the compiler walks it.

2. Derivations and parse trees

A derivation is a sequence of rewriting choices, and it makes two kinds of choice at once: which nonterminal to expand, and which production to use for it. Only the second carries information about the string’s structure. Fixing the first by convention removes the noise.

Definition (leftmost, rightmost) A derivation is leftmost if every step expands the leftmost nonterminal of the current sentential form, and rightmost if every step expands the rightmost one.

Work both on the precedence-stratified grammar introduced in §3 (repeated here so the derivations can be followed now): 

  G2 (stratified by precedence):
      E → E + T | T          an expression is a sum of terms
      T → T * F | F          a term is a product of factors
      F → ( E ) | a          a factor is atomic
Example (leftmost and rightmost derivations of a + a * a) The leftmost derivation always rewrites the underlined symbol, which is the first nonterminal from the left:

Both derivations are eight steps long and use the multiset of productions \(\{E \to E{+}T,\; E \to T,\; T \to T{*}F,\; T \to F \text{ (twice)},\; F \to \texttt{a} \text{ (three times)}\}\). What differs is only the order in which independent rewrites were performed.
Definition (parse tree) A parse tree (derivation tree) for \(G\) is an ordered tree whose root is labelled \(S\), whose internal nodes are labelled with nonterminals, whose leaves are labelled with terminals or \(\varepsilon\), and in which a node labelled \(A\) with children labelled \(X_1, \dots, X_k\) left to right requires \(A \to X_1 \cdots X_k\) to be a production of \(G\). Reading the leaves left to right gives the tree’s yield, the string it derives.
Proposition (derivations and trees) For every parse tree with yield \(w\) there is exactly one leftmost derivation of \(w\) and exactly one rightmost derivation of \(w\) corresponding to it; conversely every leftmost (rightmost) derivation determines one parse tree. The two derivations above are therefore the same tree, seen twice.
Sketch. Given a tree, expand nonterminals in the order a preorder walk that always recurses into the leftmost unexpanded child encounters them: at every stage the frontier of expanded nodes, read left to right, is a sentential form, and the next node taken is the leftmost nonterminal on that frontier, so the induced derivation is leftmost. The choice at each node is forced by the node’s children, so the derivation is unique. Rightmost is the mirror walk. Conversely, given a leftmost derivation, build the tree by induction on its length: each step replaces one leaf labelled \(A\) with children matching the production used, and leftmost order guarantees the leaf is determined at every step. ∎

3. Ambiguity, and how precedence removes it

Definition (ambiguous grammar) A grammar is ambiguous if some string in its language has two or more distinct parse trees (equivalently, two distinct leftmost derivations). A language is inherently ambiguous if every grammar for it is ambiguous; ambiguity of a grammar is a property of the grammar, and can often be engineered away.

G1 is ambiguous, and a + a * a is the shortest witness. Two leftmost derivations, differing at the very first step:

Example (two leftmost derivations, one string)

3a + E * Ea + E * E
4a + a * Ea + a * E
5a + a * aa + a * a
Same string, same grammar, different trees:

Ambiguity is not a cosmetic defect. The parse tree is the input to every later phase of a compiler, so an ambiguous grammar leaves the meaning of a program undefined. And the damage compounds: under G1 the string \(\texttt{a}\,(+\,\texttt{a})^{k}\) with \(k\) operators has \(C_k\) parse trees, the \(k\)-th Catalan number \(C_k = \frac{1}{k+1}\binom{2k}{k}\), which is \(\Theta(4^{k}/k^{3/2})\): \(1, 2, 5, 14, 42, \dots\) for \(k = 1,2,3,4,5\). Every one of those is a different program.

The fix: one nonterminal per precedence level

The repair is structural. Give each precedence level its own nonterminal, and let a level refer only to itself and to the level below. Loosest-binding operator at the top, atoms at the bottom: 

  G2:
      E → E + T | T          level 1: +   (loosest)
      T → T * F | F          level 2: *   (tighter)
      F → ( E ) | a          level 3: atoms, and the escape hatch ( E )
Proposition (G2 forces precedence and left associativity) In every parse tree of G2, no \(*\) node is an ancestor of a \(+\) node unless a parenthesized \(F \to (\,E\,)\) lies between them; and a run of equal-precedence operators groups to the left.
Precedence. A \(+\) is introduced only by \(E \to E + T\), whose children are an \(E\) and a \(T\); a \(*\) only by \(T \to T * F\), whose children are a \(T\) and an \(F\). Follow any downward path from a \(*\) node. Its subtrees are rooted at \(T\) and \(F\); from \(T\) the only productions are \(T \to T*F\) and \(T \to F\), and from \(F\) they are \(F \to \texttt{a}\) and \(F \to (\,E\,)\). So an \(E\) (the only nonterminal that can carry a \(+\)) is reachable below a \(*\) only by passing through \(F \to (\,E\,)\), that is, through explicit parentheses. Hence without parentheses every \(+\) node sits above every \(*\) node, and a bottom-up evaluator performs the multiplications first. Associativity. The rule \(E \to E + T\) is left-recursive: the recursive occurrence is the left child, and the right child \(T\) cannot itself derive an unparenthesized \(+\). So the only tree for \(\texttt{a}+\texttt{a}+\texttt{a}\) has the second \(+\) at the root with the first \(+\) inside its left subtree, giving \((\texttt{a}+\texttt{a})+\texttt{a}\). Writing the rule as \(E \to T + E\) instead would make it right-associative, which is how one writes an exponentiation level. ∎

G2 generates the same language as G1 (both describe all well-formed expressions over these five terminals) but assigns each sentence exactly one tree. Verified by exhaustive enumeration: on all 19 530 strings of length at most 6 over \(\{\texttt{a}, \texttt{+}, \texttt{*}, \texttt{(}, \texttt{)}\}\), G2 accepts exactly the same set as G1, and never with more than one parse tree.

The dangling else The other classic ambiguity, and the one every language designer meets: S → if E then S | if E then S else S | other. The string if a then if b then s1 else s2 has two trees, one attaching the else to the inner if, one to the outer. Most languages resolve it by fiat (“an else binds to the nearest unmatched then”) rather than by rewriting the grammar, because the unambiguous grammar for it is genuinely unpleasant. Languages that require the closing keyword (fi, endif) or braces do not have the problem at all: the terminator does the same job as G2’s parentheses.
Ambiguity is undecidable There is no algorithm that takes an arbitrary CFG and decides whether it is ambiguous; nor one that decides whether two CFGs generate the same language, or whether a CFG generates all of \(\Sigma^{*}\). These follow from the undecidability of the Post correspondence problem, and they sit alongside the halting problem in the landscape sketched in P, NP & NP-Completeness. In practice a parser generator does not decide ambiguity: it checks a sufficient condition (that its parse table has no conflicts), and reports a conflict when the check fails.

4. Regular \(\subsetneq\) context-free

Two claims, proved separately: every regular language is context-free, and some context-free language is not regular. Together they place the regular languages strictly inside the context-free ones.

Theorem (containment) Every regular language is context-free.
Proof (construction). Let \(L\) be regular. By Kleene’s theorem (see Regular Expressions & Automata) there is a deterministic finite automaton (DFA) \(M = (Q, \Sigma, \delta, q_0, F)\) with \(L(M) = L\). Build a grammar with one nonterminal \(A_q\) per state \(q \in Q\), start symbol \(A_{q_0}\), and productions \[ A_q \to c\,A_{\delta(q,c)} \ \text{ for every } q \in Q,\, c \in \Sigma; \qquad A_q \to \varepsilon \ \text{ for every } q \in F. \] Every production has at most one nonterminal, at its right end (such a grammar is called right-linear), so it is a CFG. By induction on \(|w|\): \(A_q \Rightarrow^{*} w\) if and only if \(M\) started in state \(q\) reaches an accepting state after reading \(w\). The base case \(w = \varepsilon\) is the rule \(A_q \to \varepsilon\), present exactly when \(q \in F\). For the step, \(w = c\,w'\) forces the first rewrite to be \(A_q \to c\,A_{\delta(q,c)}\), the only production emitting \(c\) from \(A_q\), and the claim follows from the hypothesis applied to \(w'\) at state \(\delta(q,c)\). Taking \(q = q_0\) gives \(L(G) = L(M) = L\). ∎
Example (the construction run) The language “an even number of As” over \(\{\texttt{A},\texttt{B}\}\) has a two-state DFA: \(q_0\) (even, and accepting), \(q_1\) (odd), with \(\delta(q_0,\texttt{A}) = q_1\), \(\delta(q_1,\texttt{A}) = q_0\), and \(\texttt{B}\) a self-loop on both. The construction gives, mechanically: 
  A₀ → A A₁ | B A₀ | ε      state q0: even, accepting
  A₁ → A A₀ | B A₁          state q1: odd, not accepting
A derivation of ABAB: \(A_0 \Rightarrow \texttt{A}A_1 \Rightarrow \texttt{AB}A_1 \Rightarrow \texttt{ABA}A_0 \Rightarrow \texttt{ABAB}A_0 \Rightarrow \texttt{ABAB}\), ending on the \(\varepsilon\) rule because \(q_0\) accepts. Generating every string of length at most 4 from this grammar produces exactly the 16 strings with an even number of As, and no others.

The containment is not an accident of the construction: a right-linear grammar can only ever carry one nonterminal in its sentential form, and that nonterminal is exactly the automaton’s current state. A general CFG may carry many nonterminals at once, and that is the extra power.

The separating language

Definition (the language \(\texttt{a}^n\texttt{b}^n\)) \(L_{ab} = \{\, \texttt{a}^{n}\texttt{b}^{n} : n \ge 0 \,\}\): \(\varepsilon\), ab, aabb, aaabbb, and so on. Matching counts, nothing else.
Proposition (\(L_{ab}\) is context-free) The two-production grammar \(S \to \texttt{a}\,S\,\texttt{b} \mid \varepsilon\) generates exactly \(L_{ab}\), and is unambiguous.
Every sentential form reachable from \(S\) is \(\texttt{a}^{k}S\texttt{b}^{k}\) or \(\texttt{a}^{k}\texttt{b}^{k}\) (induction on the number of steps: the first rule preserves the shape and increments \(k\), the second terminates it), so \(L(G) \subseteq L_{ab}\); and \(\texttt{a}^{n}\texttt{b}^{n}\) is produced by \(n\) uses of the first rule and one of the second, so \(L_{ab} \subseteq L(G)\). Uniqueness: for a nonempty target the first rule is the only one whose yield is nonempty, so at every stage the choice is forced by whether any characters remain. The recursion is the counter: the \(k\) pending bs to the right of \(S\) remember how many as have been emitted. ∎
Lemma (pumping lemma for regular languages) If \(L\) is regular, there is an integer \(p \ge 1\) (a pumping length) such that every \(s \in L\) with \(|s| \ge p\) can be written \(s = xyz\) with (i) \(|y| \ge 1\), (ii) \(|xy| \le p\), and (iii) \(x y^{i} z \in L\) for every \(i \ge 0\).
Take a DFA for \(L\) with \(p\) states, and let \(s = s_1 s_2 \cdots s_m \in L\) with \(m \ge p\). Running the DFA on \(s\) visits states \(r_0, r_1, \dots, r_m\), one more than the number of characters read. Among the first \(p+1\) of these, \(r_0, \dots, r_p\), there are \(p+1\) entries drawn from only \(p\) states, so by the pigeonhole principle two coincide: \(r_j = r_k\) for some \(0 \le j < k \le p\). Set \(x = s_1 \cdots s_j\), \(y = s_{j+1} \cdots s_k\), \(z = s_{k+1} \cdots s_m\). Then \(|y| = k - j \ge 1\) and \(|xy| = k \le p\), giving (i) and (ii). For (iii): reading \(y\) takes the machine from \(r_j\) back to the same state \(r_j\), so that loop may be traversed any number of times (including zero) without changing where the machine ends up; since reading \(z\) from \(r_j\) reaches the accepting state \(r_m\), every \(xy^{i}z\) is accepted. ∎
Theorem (\(L_{ab}\) is not regular) \(L_{ab} = \{\texttt{a}^{n}\texttt{b}^{n}\}\) is not a regular language. Hence the regular languages are a strict subset of the context-free languages.
Suppose \(L_{ab}\) were regular, and let \(p\) be a pumping length for it. The string \(s = \texttt{a}^{p}\texttt{b}^{p}\) is in \(L_{ab}\) and has length \(2p \ge p\), so the lemma applies: \(s = xyz\) with \(|y| \ge 1\) and \(|xy| \le p\). Because the first \(p\) characters of \(s\) are all as and \(xy\) lies within them, \(y\) consists solely of as, say \(y = \texttt{a}^{k}\) with \(1 \le k \le p\). Pump once (\(i = 2\)): \(x y^{2} z = \texttt{a}^{p+k}\texttt{b}^{p}\), which the lemma says must be in \(L_{ab}\). But \(p + k \neq p\) since \(k \ge 1\), so it has unequal counts and is not in \(L_{ab}\): contradiction. Therefore \(L_{ab}\) is not regular. Combined with the previous proposition it is context-free but not regular, and combined with the containment theorem the inclusion regular \(\subseteq\) context-free is proper. ∎

The intuition the proof makes precise: a finite automaton’s only memory is which state it is in, and there are finitely many states, so after reading enough as it must revisit one and thereby lose track of how many it has seen. A grammar has no such bound, because a sentential form can grow: the pending \(\texttt{b}^{k}\) in \(\texttt{a}^{k}S\texttt{b}^{k}\) is an unbounded counter written down in the middle of the work.

Aside: pushdown automata, and what the stack buys The machine that matches a grammar the way a finite automaton matches a regex is the pushdown automaton (PDA): a finite automaton plus one unbounded stack. A move reads (or skips) an input character, pops the top stack symbol, and pushes a string of symbols in its place; acceptance is by final state or by empty stack. The theorem parallel to Kleene’s holds: a language is context-free if and only if some (nondeterministic) PDA accepts it, and the translation is direct: put the start symbol on the stack, and thereafter replace a nonterminal on top by the right-hand side of one of its productions, or match a terminal on top against the next input character. 
  PDA for aⁿbⁿ, in three rules:
      read 'a'  →  push X            count up, one X per a
      read 'b'  →  pop  X            count down; fail if the stack is empty
      end       →  accept iff the stack is empty
On aaabbb the stack climbs to XXX and drains to empty: accept. On aabbb the third b finds an empty stack: reject. On aaabb the stack still holds one X at end of input: reject. The stack is precisely what the regular-language proof showed a finite automaton lacks, and precisely what §5’s recursive-descent parser gets for free: its call stack is the PDA’s stack, and the depth of parenthesis nesting is the depth of recursion.

One asymmetry worth knowing: for finite automata, nondeterminism adds no power (the subset construction determinizes any nondeterministic finite automaton). For pushdown automata it does: the deterministic PDAs accept a strictly smaller class than the CFGs describe. That gap is why practical parser generators (§7) accept only a restricted family of grammars, and why the fully general parser of §6 costs cubic time.

Adding memory is the organizing principle of the whole ladder, the Chomsky hierarchy:

A compiler straddles the first two rungs on purpose. Its lexer is a finite automaton, because identifiers, numbers and keywords are regular and a linear scan is the cheapest thing available; its parser is a pushdown machine, because nested expressions and blocks are not regular. The type checker then handles what neither can (“is this variable declared?” is not context-free), which is why the front end has three phases and not one.

5. Recursive-descent parsing

The most direct parser writes one function per nonterminal. The function for \(A\) is responsible for consuming an \(A\) from the front of the input; where the grammar has \(A \to B\,C\) the function calls the functions for \(B\) and \(C\) in order; where the grammar has a terminal, the function matches and consumes it. The grammar becomes the call graph, and mutual recursion (see Recursion & Recurrences) does the nesting.

The obstacle: left recursion

Translating G2 naively fails immediately. The rule \(E \to E + T\) says “to parse an \(E\), first parse an \(E\)”, so parseE() would call parseE() with the input pointer unmoved: infinite recursion, stack overflow, no characters consumed. Any grammar with \(A \Rightarrow^{*} A\alpha\) is left-recursive and cannot be parsed by naive top-down descent.

The standard repair rewrites each left-recursive level as a loop. \(E \to E + T \mid T\) generates exactly a \(T\) followed by zero or more + T groups, so write it in extended notation with braces meaning “zero or more”: 

  G3 (G2 with the left recursion turned into iteration; same language, same trees):
      E → T { + T }
      T → F { * F }
      F → ( E ) | a

The loop must build its tree by folding left (node = combine(node, next) inside the loop body), which reproduces G2’s left associativity exactly; folding right instead would silently change the grammar’s meaning. This is the one place the transformation can go wrong, and the code below marks it.

The parser

The tokens are a string of characters here, with a sentinel $ appended so the lookahead is always defined; a real front end feeds a token stream from the lexer instead. Each function returns the subtree it parsed, printed as a fully parenthesized expression so the structure is visible. 

// Recursive descent for  E -> T { + T }   T -> F { * F }   F -> ( E ) | a
// One function per nonterminal; the call stack is the pushdown store.
// Returns a fully parenthesized string so the tree shape is visible.
struct Parser {
    string s;                      // input, with '$' appended as sentinel
    size_t p = 0;                  // read position: never moves backward

    char la() const { return s[p]; }                 // one-token lookahead
    void eat(char c) {                               // match and consume
        if (s[p] != c) throw runtime_error("expected " + string(1, c));
        p++;
    }

    string E() {                                     // E -> T { + T }
        string v = T();
        while (la() == '+') {                        // LOOP replaces E -> E + T
            eat('+');
            v = "(" + v + " + " + T() + ")";         // fold LEFT: left-associative
        }
        return v;
    }
    string T() {                                     // T -> F { * F }
        string v = F();
        while (la() == '*') {                        // LOOP replaces T -> T * F
            eat('*');
            v = "(" + v + " * " + F() + ")";         // fold LEFT
        }
        return v;
    }
    string F() {                                     // F -> ( E ) | a
        if (la() == '(') {                           // case 1: parenthesized
            eat('('); string v = E(); eat(')');
            return v;
        }
        eat('a');                                    // case 2: atom
        return "a";
    }
};

string parse(string text) {
    Parser ps{text + "$"};
    string v = ps.E();
    if (ps.la() != '$') throw runtime_error("trailing input");  // must consume all
    return v;
}
// Recursive descent for  E -> T { + T }   T -> F { * F }   F -> ( E ) | a
// One method per nonterminal; the call stack is the pushdown store.
class Parser {
    private final String s;        // input, with '$' appended as sentinel
    private int p = 0;             // read position: never moves backward

    Parser(String text) { this.s = text + "$"; }

    private char la() { return s.charAt(p); }        // one-token lookahead
    private void eat(char c) {                       // match and consume
        if (s.charAt(p) != c) throw new IllegalArgumentException("expected " + c);
        p++;
    }

    String E() {                                     // E -> T { + T }
        String v = T();
        while (la() == '+') {                        // LOOP replaces E -> E + T
            eat('+');
            v = "(" + v + " + " + T() + ")";         // fold LEFT: left-associative
        }
        return v;
    }
    String T() {                                     // T -> F { * F }
        String v = F();
        while (la() == '*') {                        // LOOP replaces T -> T * F
            eat('*');
            v = "(" + v + " * " + F() + ")";         // fold LEFT
        }
        return v;
    }
    String F() {                                     // F -> ( E ) | a
        if (la() == '(') {                           // case 1: parenthesized
            eat('('); String v = E(); eat(')');
            return v;
        }
        eat('a');                                    // case 2: atom
        return "a";
    }
    static String parse(String text) {
        Parser ps = new Parser(text);
        String v = ps.E();
        if (ps.la() != '$') throw new IllegalArgumentException("trailing input");
        return v;
    }
}
# Recursive descent for  E -> T { + T }   T -> F { * F }   F -> ( E ) | a
# One function per nonterminal; the call stack is the pushdown store.
class Parser:
    def __init__(self, text):
        self.s = text + "$"        # sentinel so lookahead is always defined
        self.p = 0                 # read position: never moves backward

    def la(self):                                    # one-token lookahead
        return self.s[self.p]

    def eat(self, c):                                # match and consume
        if self.s[self.p] != c:
            raise ValueError("expected " + c)
        self.p += 1

    def E(self):                                     # E -> T { + T }
        v = self.T()
        while self.la() == '+':                      # LOOP replaces E -> E + T
            self.eat('+')
            v = "(" + v + " + " + self.T() + ")"     # fold LEFT: left-associative
        return v

    def T(self):                                     # T -> F { * F }
        v = self.F()
        while self.la() == '*':                      # LOOP replaces T -> T * F
            self.eat('*')
            v = "(" + v + " * " + self.F() + ")"     # fold LEFT
        return v

    def F(self):                                     # F -> ( E ) | a
        if self.la() == '(':                         # case 1: parenthesized
            self.eat('(')
            v = self.E()
            self.eat(')')
            return v
        self.eat('a')                                # case 2: atom
        return "a"

def parse(text):
    ps = Parser(text)
    v = ps.E()
    if ps.la() != '$':                               # must consume all input
        raise ValueError("trailing input")
    return v
// Recursive descent for  E -> T { + T }   T -> F { * F }   F -> ( E ) | a
// One method per nonterminal; the call stack is the pushdown store.
struct Parser { s: Vec<u8>, p: usize }               // s ends with b'$'

impl Parser {
    fn la(&self) -> u8 { self.s[self.p] }             // one-token lookahead
    fn eat(&mut self, c: u8) -> Result<(), String> {  // match and consume
        if self.s[self.p] != c { return Err(format!("expected {}", c as char)); }
        self.p += 1;
        Ok(())
    }

    fn e(&mut self) -> Result<String, String> {       // E -> T { + T }
        let mut v = self.t()?;
        while self.la() == b'+' {                     // LOOP replaces E -> E + T
            self.eat(b'+')?;
            v = format!("({} + {})", v, self.t()?);   // fold LEFT: left-associative
        }
        Ok(v)
    }
    fn t(&mut self) -> Result<String, String> {       // T -> F { * F }
        let mut v = self.f()?;
        while self.la() == b'*' {                     // LOOP replaces T -> T * F
            self.eat(b'*')?;
            v = format!("({} * {})", v, self.f()?);   // fold LEFT
        }
        Ok(v)
    }
    fn f(&mut self) -> Result<String, String> {       // F -> ( E ) | a
        if self.la() == b'(' {                        // case 1: parenthesized
            self.eat(b'(')?;
            let v = self.e()?;
            self.eat(b')')?;
            return Ok(v);
        }
        self.eat(b'a')?;                              // case 2: atom
        Ok("a".to_string())
    }
}

fn parse(text: &str) -> Result<String, String> {
    let mut ps = Parser { s: format!("{}$", text).into_bytes(), p: 0 };
    let v = ps.e()?;
    if ps.la() != b'$' { return Err("trailing input".into()); }   // consume all
    Ok(v)
}

Cost

Proposition (recursive descent runs in \(\Theta(n)\)) On an input of \(n\) tokens the parser above performs at most \(3n + 3\) function calls and exactly \(n\) token consumptions, for \(\Theta(n)\) time and \(\Theta(d)\) stack space where \(d\) is the maximum nesting depth of parentheses (\(d \le n\)).
Charge each call to a token. A call to \(F\) is made either from a \(T\) (at most one per \(T\) call) or immediately after consuming a *; a call to \(T\) either from an \(E\) or after consuming a +; a call to \(E\) either once at the top level or immediately after consuming a (. So \(\#E \le 1 + \#\texttt{(}\), \(\#T \le \#E + \#\texttt{+}\), and \(\#F \le \#T + \#\texttt{*}\); each of these token classes is disjoint and each token is consumed once, so summing gives \(\#E + \#T + \#F \le 3 + 3n\). Between calls each function does \(O(1)\) work per loop iteration, and each iteration consumes an operator token, so the total non-call work is \(O(n)\) as well. Consumptions: eat advances pos by one and pos never decreases, so there are exactly \(n\) of them on a successful parse. Stack depth: a frame is pushed only for a nonterminal, and the chain \(E \to T \to F\) adds a constant 3 per level of parenthesis nesting, so depth is \(\Theta(d)\). ∎

Two costs are worth naming. The linear bound depends on the single-token lookahead being decisive: at every choice point (which case of \(F\), whether to enter a loop) one token settles the question with no guessing and no backtracking. A grammar with that property is called LL(1) (scanned Left to right, producing a Leftmost derivation, with 1 token of lookahead). When a grammar is not LL(1), a hand-written descent parser backtracks, and the worst case degrades to exponential. And the stack depth is real: deeply nested input can overflow the machine stack, which is why production parsers for adversarial input either impose a nesting limit or manage an explicit stack instead of the call stack.

6. CYK: parsing by dynamic programming

Recursive descent is fast but picky: it needs an LL(1) grammar, and it cannot handle left recursion or ambiguity at all. The Cocke–Younger–Kasami algorithm (CYK) gives up the linear time and takes in exchange a parser that works on every context-free grammar, ambiguous or not. It is dynamic programming on substrings: the subproblem is “which nonterminals derive \(w_i \cdots w_j\)?”, and the answer for a long substring is assembled from the answers for its pieces.

Chomsky Normal Form

CYK requires productions of a fixed shape, so that splitting a substring in two is always the right move.

Definition (Chomsky Normal Form) A grammar is in Chomsky Normal Form (CNF) if every production has one of the two shapes \[ A \to BC \quad (B, C \text{ nonterminals}), \qquad A \to c \quad (c \text{ a terminal}), \] plus \(S \to \varepsilon\) if the empty string is in the language. Every CFG can be converted to CNF generating the same language (minus \(\varepsilon\) handling), by four mechanical steps: give each terminal appearing in a long rule its own nonterminal; eliminate \(\varepsilon\)-productions; eliminate unit productions \(A \to B\) by splicing in \(B\)’s right-hand sides; and binarize rules longer than two symbols by introducing fresh intermediate nonterminals. The conversion at most squares the grammar size.
Example (G2 converted to CNF, step by step) Start from \(E \to E{+}T \mid T\), \(T \to T{*}F \mid F\), \(F \to (\,E\,) \mid \texttt{a}\).
  1. Isolate terminals. Introduce \(P \to \texttt{+}\), \(M \to \texttt{*}\), \(L \to \texttt{(}\), \(R \to \texttt{)}\), and rewrite the long rules over them: \(E \to E\,P\,T\), \(T \to T\,M\,F\), \(F \to L\,E\,R\).
  2. Eliminate unit productions. The unit rules are \(E \to T\) and \(T \to F\), so the unit closure of \(E\) is \(\{E, T, F\}\) and of \(T\) is \(\{T, F\}\). Give each nonterminal every non-unit right-hand side reachable through units: \(E \to E\,P\,T \mid T\,M\,F \mid L\,E\,R \mid \texttt{a}\), \(T \to T\,M\,F \mid L\,E\,R \mid \texttt{a}\), \(F \to L\,E\,R \mid \texttt{a}\).
  3. Binarize. Each remaining three-symbol rule gets one fresh nonterminal for its first two symbols: \(E_1 \to E\,P\), \(T_1 \to T\,M\), \(F_1 \to E\,R\). (The last is paired from the right, so \(L\,E\,R\) becomes \(L\,F_1\).)
  4. There are no \(\varepsilon\)-productions to remove. 
  G2 in Chomsky Normal Form (10 nonterminals, 15 productions):
      E  → E₁ T | T₁ F | L F₁ | a
      T  → T₁ F | L F₁ | a
      F  → L F₁ | a
      E₁ → E P          T₁ → T M          F₁ → E R
      P  → +            M  → *            L  → (            R → )
Checked by exhaustive enumeration over all 19 530 strings of length at most 6 in these five terminals: this CNF grammar accepts exactly the same set as G2, with zero mismatches.

The algorithm

Index the input \(w = w_1 w_2 \cdots w_n\) from 1. Define the table \[ \mathrm{tab}[i][j] \;=\; \{\, A \in V : A \Rightarrow^{*} w_i w_{i+1} \cdots w_j \,\}, \] the set of nonterminals deriving the substring from \(i\) to \(j\) inclusive. Two recurrences fill it: \[ \mathrm{tab}[i][i] = \{\, A : A \to w_i \in R \,\}, \] \[ \mathrm{tab}[i][j] = \bigcup_{k=i}^{\,j-1} \{\, A : A \to BC \in R,\ B \in \mathrm{tab}[i][k],\ C \in \mathrm{tab}[k{+}1][j] \,\}. \] The second is exactly the CNF shape read as a subproblem decomposition: if \(A\) derives \(w_i \cdots w_j\), the first rewrite must be some \(A \to BC\), and \(B\) must cover a prefix \(w_i \cdots w_k\) with \(C\) covering the rest. Every \(k\) from \(i\) to \(j-1\) is a candidate split point, so all are tried. The string is in the language exactly when \(S \in \mathrm{tab}[1][n]\). Because the recurrence for a span of length \(\ell\) reads only spans of length \(< \ell\), filling the table by increasing length is a valid evaluation order, and each cell is computed once: the two hallmarks of dynamic programming. The split-point loop is the same shape as the one in Matrix Chain Multiplication, and for the same reason: both problems ask how to cut an interval in two. 

// CYK recognition. Grammar in Chomsky Normal Form:
//   unit[A]   = set of terminals c with A -> c
//   pairs[A]  = list of (B, C) with A -> B C
// tab[i][j] holds every nonterminal deriving w[i..j] (1-based, inclusive).
bool cyk(const vector<char>& w,
         const map<string, set<char>>& unit,
         const map<string, vector<pair<string,string>>>& pairs,
         const string& start) {
    int n = w.size();
    vector<vector<set<string>>> tab(n + 1, vector<set<string>>(n + 1));

    for (int i = 1; i <= n; i++)                    // spans of length 1
        for (auto& [A, cs] : unit)
            if (cs.count(w[i - 1])) tab[i][i].insert(A);

    for (int len = 2; len <= n; len++)              // increasing span length
        for (int i = 1; i + len - 1 <= n; i++) {
            int j = i + len - 1;
            for (int k = i; k < j; k++)             // every split point
                for (auto& [A, rhs] : pairs)
                    for (auto& [B, C] : rhs)
                        if (tab[i][k].count(B) && tab[k + 1][j].count(C))
                            tab[i][j].insert(A);
        }
    return tab[1][n].count(start) > 0;              // start symbol spans it all
}
// CYK recognition. Grammar in Chomsky Normal Form:
//   unit.get(A)  = set of terminals c with A -> c
//   pairs.get(A) = list of (B, C) with A -> B C
// tab[i][j] holds every nonterminal deriving w[i..j] (1-based, inclusive).
static boolean cyk(char[] w,
                   Map<String, Set<Character>> unit,
                   Map<String, List<String[]>> pairs,
                   String start) {
    int n = w.length;
    Set<String>[][] tab = new HashSet[n + 1][n + 1];
    for (int i = 0; i <= n; i++)
        for (int j = 0; j <= n; j++) tab[i][j] = new HashSet<>();

    for (int i = 1; i <= n; i++)                    // spans of length 1
        for (var e : unit.entrySet())
            if (e.getValue().contains(w[i - 1])) tab[i][i].add(e.getKey());

    for (int len = 2; len <= n; len++)              // increasing span length
        for (int i = 1; i + len - 1 <= n; i++) {
            int j = i + len - 1;
            for (int k = i; k < j; k++)             // every split point
                for (var e : pairs.entrySet())
                    for (String[] bc : e.getValue())
                        if (tab[i][k].contains(bc[0]) && tab[k + 1][j].contains(bc[1]))
                            tab[i][j].add(e.getKey());
        }
    return tab[1][n].contains(start);               // start symbol spans it all
}
# CYK recognition. Grammar in Chomsky Normal Form:
#   unit[A]  = set of terminals c with A -> c
#   pairs[A] = list of (B, C) with A -> B C
# tab[i][j] holds every nonterminal deriving w[i..j] (1-based, inclusive).
def cyk(w, unit, pairs, start):
    n = len(w)
    tab = [[set() for _ in range(n + 1)] for _ in range(n + 1)]

    for i in range(1, n + 1):                       # spans of length 1
        for A, cs in unit.items():
            if w[i - 1] in cs:
                tab[i][i].add(A)

    for length in range(2, n + 1):                  # increasing span length
        for i in range(1, n - length + 2):
            j = i + length - 1
            for k in range(i, j):                   # every split point
                for A, rhs in pairs.items():
                    for (B, C) in rhs:
                        if B in tab[i][k] and C in tab[k + 1][j]:
                            tab[i][j].add(A)

    return start in tab[1][n]                       # start symbol spans it all
// CYK recognition. Grammar in Chomsky Normal Form:
//   unit[A]  = set of terminals c with A -> c
//   pairs[A] = list of (B, C) with A -> B C
// tab[i][j] holds every nonterminal deriving w[i..j] (1-based, inclusive).
use std::collections::{HashMap, HashSet};

fn cyk(w: &[char],
       unit: &HashMap<String, HashSet<char>>,
       pairs: &HashMap<String, Vec<(String, String)>>,
       start: &str) -> bool {
    let n = w.len();
    let mut tab = vec![vec![HashSet::<String>::new(); n + 1]; n + 1];

    for i in 1..=n {                                 // spans of length 1
        for (a, cs) in unit {
            if cs.contains(&w[i - 1]) { tab[i][i].insert(a.clone()); }
        }
    }
    for len in 2..=n {                               // increasing span length
        for i in 1..=(n - len + 1) {
            let j = i + len - 1;
            for k in i..j {                          // every split point
                let mut found = Vec::new();
                for (a, rhs) in pairs {
                    for (b, c) in rhs {
                        if tab[i][k].contains(b) && tab[k + 1][j].contains(c) {
                            found.push(a.clone());
                        }
                    }
                }
                for a in found { tab[i][j].insert(a); }
            }
        }
    }
    tab[1][n].contains(start)                        // start symbol spans it all
}

Reading the table upward, cell by cell:

  • Length 1. Each a gets \(\{E, T, F\}\) (all three have the production \(\to \texttt{a}\)); + gets \(\{P\}\), * gets \(\{M\}\).
  • Length 2. Span (1,2) = a +: the only pair rule whose right half can be \(P\) is \(E_1 \to E\,P\), and \(E \in \mathrm{tab}[1][1]\), so \(E_1\) goes in. Span (3,4) = a * gets \(T_1\) by \(T_1 \to T\,M\) for the same reason. Spans (2,3) = + a and (4,5) = * a are empty: no production starts with \(P\) or \(M\), so nothing derives an operator followed by an operand.
  • Length 3. Span (1,3) = a + a: split \(k = 1\) pairs \(\{E,T,F\}\) with the empty cell (2,3) and yields nothing; split \(k = 2\) pairs \(E_1 \in \mathrm{tab}[1][2]\) with \(T \in \mathrm{tab}[3][3]\), matching \(E \to E_1 T\), so \(E\) goes in. Span (3,5) = a * a: split \(k = 4\) pairs \(T_1 \in \mathrm{tab}[3][4]\) with \(F \in \mathrm{tab}[5][5]\), matching both \(E \to T_1 F\) and \(T \to T_1 F\), so \(\{E, T\}\) goes in. Span (2,4) = + a * stays empty.
  • Length 4. Both cells are empty. Span (1,4) = a + a * tries \(k = 1, 2, 3\), pairing \((\{E,T,F\}, \emptyset)\), then \((\{E_1\}, \{T_1\})\), then \((\{E\}, \{M\})\). The first has an empty partner. The second finds no production with right-hand side \(E_1 T_1\) (the rule \(E \to E_1 T\) wants a \(T\) on the right, and \(T_1\) is a different symbol). The third finds no production \(E\,M\) (the rule \(T_1 \to T\,M\) wants a \(T\) on the left, and \(\mathrm{tab}[1][3] = \{E\}\) does not contain one). Span (2,5) likewise. Empty cells are informative: a trailing operator can never complete an expression.
  • Length 5. Four split points. \(k = 1\) and \(k = 3\) and \(k = 4\) all pair something with an empty cell. Only \(k = 2\) works: \(E_1 \in \mathrm{tab}[1][2]\) (spanning a +) with \(T \in \mathrm{tab}[3][5]\) (spanning a * a), matching \(E \to E_1 T\). So \(E \in \mathrm{tab}[1][5]\): accepted.

Note what the successful split says. The only way the whole string parses is with a * a as a single \(T\) on the right of the +. The table has rediscovered precedence, and the winning split point is the operator at the root of the parse tree in §2. Ten of the fifteen cells are nonempty; the five empty ones are exactly the substrings that begin or end with a dangling operator. The same code rejects a + * a and a a (the start symbol never reaches the top cell) and accepts a + a, ( a + a ) * a.

Proposition (CYK cost) On an input of length \(n\) with a CNF grammar of \(|G|\) productions, CYK runs in \(\Theta(n^{3}\,|G|)\) time and \(\Theta(n^{2}|V|)\) space.
The table has one cell per substring: \(\binom{n+1}{2} = \Theta(n^{2})\) of them, each a subset of \(V\), giving the space bound. Filling a cell \(\mathrm{tab}[i][j]\) tries every split point \(k \in \{i, \dots, j-1\}\), at most \(n-1\), and for each split examines every production \(A \to BC\), at most \(|G|\), doing \(O(1)\) work per (set membership on a bitset over \(V\)). So the total is \(\Theta(n^{2}) \cdot O(n\,|G|) = O(n^{3}|G|)\). The bound is tight on grammars where most cells fill: the triple loop over \((i, j, k)\) executes \(\sum_{\ell=2}^{n}(n-\ell+1)(\ell-1) = \binom{n+1}{3} = \Theta(n^{3})\) times regardless of the input, since nothing prunes it. At \(n = 5\) that is \(\binom{6}{3} = 20\) split-point evaluations, matching the counts listed cell by cell above. ∎

The grammar factor \(|G|\) is a constant for a fixed language, but it is not a small one after CNF conversion (which can square the grammar), and it is the reason CYK is a theoretical workhorse rather than a production parser. What earns its place is generality: it parses any CFG, including ambiguous ones, and storing a back-pointer with each entry (which production and which split point put \(A\) into \(\mathrm{tab}[i][j]\)) turns recognition into parsing by the usual traceback: follow the back-pointers down from \(S \in \mathrm{tab}[1][n]\) to rebuild the tree. Storing all back-pointers rather than one represents every parse tree at once, in \(O(n^{3})\) space, which is how parsers for natural language handle grammars with thousands of trees per sentence.

7. Parser generators, and where this sits

Neither parser above is what a compiler usually ships. Between LL(1) recursive descent (linear, but only for grammars a single lookahead token can steer) and CYK (any grammar, but cubic) sits the family that real tools use: bottom-up LR parsers, which shift input symbols onto a stack and reduce the top of the stack by a production when a right-hand side is complete. LR parsers run in \(\Theta(n)\), handle left recursion natively (the natural direction for a bottom-up method), and accept a strictly larger class of grammars than LL(1).

A parser generator takes the grammar as its input file and emits the parser: yacc and its descendant bison (LALR(1), C), ANTLR (LL(*), several target languages), CUP (Java), lalrpop (Rust). Writing the grammar is then the whole job, and the grammar file is the language specification. What such a tool actually computes is a table of shift and reduce actions indexed by state and lookahead; where the construction wants to put two different actions in one entry it reports a shift/reduce or reduce/reduce conflict. Those conflicts are what the ambiguity of §3 looks like at the tool level: a shift/reduce conflict is the exact symptom of the dangling-else grammar, and the conventional resolution (prefer shift) implements “bind else to the nearest then”. Because ambiguity itself is undecidable, a conflict report is a conservative warning, not a proof of ambiguity: some conflict-free rewriting may exist.

Hand-written recursive descent has not gone away, though: several production compilers (including major C++ and C# front ends) use it, because a hand-written parser gives far better control over error messages and error recovery than a generated table does, and because the code reads like the grammar it implements.

Under G1 all four operands and three binary operators are interchangeable as far as the grammar is concerned: the grammar is exactly “a binary tree over four leaves whose internal nodes take the operators in left-to-right order”, and the number of such shapes is the Catalan number \(C_3 = \frac{1}{4}\binom{6}{3} = 5\). (The counts for \(k = 1,2,3,4,5\) operators are \(1, 2, 5, 14, 42\).) Under G2 the stratification proposition of §3 forces the unique tree \(((\texttt{a} + (\texttt{a} * \texttt{a})) + \texttt{a})\).
  3. Extend G2 with unary minus and exponentiation so that - binds tighter than *, ^ binds tightest of all, and ^ is right-associative (so a^a^a means a^(a^a)). Write the grammar, and say which of your rules a recursive-descent parser must handle with a loop and which with a recursive call.
    solution Add a level per operator, tightest at the bottom: 
      E → E + T | T
  T → T * U | U
  U → - U | B          unary minus: recursive, so --a parses
  B → F ^ B | F          RIGHT-recursive: right-associative
  F → ( E ) | a
    In the parser: \(E\) and \(T\) are left-recursive, so each becomes a loop with a left fold (while la()=='+', while la()=='*'). \(U\) is left-most recursive only through the terminal -, which is consumed before the recursive call, so a plain recursive call works and no transformation is needed. \(B\) is right-recursive, and right recursion is harmless for top-down parsing: parse an \(F\), and if the lookahead is ^, consume it and call \(B\) recursively, combining as F ^ (result). That recursive call is what makes it right-associative; writing it as a loop with a left fold would give (a^a)^a and change the language it means.
  4. Prove that \(L = \{\, w \in \{\texttt{a},\texttt{b}\}^{*} : w \text{ has equally many } \texttt{a}\text{s and } \texttt{b}\text{s} \,\}\) is not regular, and give a CFG for it.
    solution Not regular. Let \(p\) be a pumping length and take \(s = \texttt{a}^{p}\texttt{b}^{p} \in L\), of length \(2p \ge p\). Write \(s = xyz\) with \(|y| \ge 1\) and \(|xy| \le p\); since the first \(p\) characters are all as, \(y = \texttt{a}^{k}\) for some \(k \ge 1\). Then \(xy^{2}z = \texttt{a}^{p+k}\texttt{b}^{p}\) has \(p+k\) as and \(p\) bs, so it is not in \(L\), contradicting the lemma. (An alternative: if \(L\) were regular then \(L \cap L(\texttt{a*b*}) = \{\texttt{a}^{n}\texttt{b}^{n}\}\) would be regular too, since regular languages are closed under intersection, and §4 showed it is not.) Grammar. \(S \to \texttt{a}\,S\,\texttt{b} \mid \texttt{b}\,S\,\texttt{a} \mid S\,S \mid \varepsilon\). Every production keeps the counts balanced, so everything derived is in \(L\); conversely any balanced string either has a proper balanced prefix (use \(S \to SS\)) or is \(\texttt{a}\,u\,\texttt{b}\) or \(\texttt{b}\,u\,\texttt{a}\) with \(u\) balanced (use the first two rules), so induction on length gives the converse. Note this grammar is ambiguous (abab parses both as \(SS\) and as \(\texttt{a}(\texttt{b}\,\varepsilon\,\texttt{a})\texttt{b}\)); the language is not inherently ambiguous, but this grammar for it is.
  5. The pumping lemma is a one-way street: it is a property of every regular language, not a characterization of them. Explain precisely what conclusion you may and may not draw when a language satisfies the lemma’s conclusion, and why the proof of §4 is nevertheless valid.
    solution The lemma reads “\(L\) regular \(\Rightarrow\) \(L\) is pumpable”. Its contrapositive, “\(L\) not pumpable \(\Rightarrow\) \(L\) not regular”, is what §4 uses, and that is valid. The converse is false: there exist non-regular languages that are pumpable, so exhibiting a valid decomposition proves nothing about regularity. Practically, this means the lemma is usable only in one direction, and the proof has a fixed adversarial shape: the pumping length \(p\) is given to you (you may not choose it), you choose the string \(s \in L\) with \(|s| \ge p\), the decomposition \(xyz\) is given to you subject only to \(|y| \ge 1\) and \(|xy| \le p\), and you choose the exponent \(i\). Losing track of who chooses what is the usual error. In §4 the choice \(s = \texttt{a}^{p}\texttt{b}^{p}\) is what makes the constraint \(|xy| \le p\) do the work, by trapping \(y\) inside the as no matter how the adversary splits.
  6. Run the recursive-descent parser of §5 by hand on the input a * ( a + a and say exactly where and how it fails. Then do the same for a + a ). Why do the two failures surface in different places in the code?
    solution a * ( a + a: \(E\) calls \(T\) calls \(F\), which eats a; back in \(T\) the lookahead is *, so the loop is entered, * is consumed and \(F\) is called at position 2. Lookahead is (, so case 1 runs: ( is consumed, \(E\) is called, parses a + a, and returns with the lookahead at the sentinel $. \(F\) then calls eat(')'), which finds $ instead: the parser throws from inside eat, with the useful message “expected )” and the position of the failure. a + a ): everything parses normally up to position 3; the \(E\)-loop exits because the lookahead ) is not +, \(E\) returns, and the top-level check la() != '$' fires: “trailing input”. The difference is structural. A missing closer is detected by a specific eat that knows which terminal it wanted, because the grammar told it. An extra closer matches no pending expectation at all, so no eat is looking for it; only the final “all input consumed” check catches it. This is why that final check is not optional: without it the parser would happily accept any string with a valid expression as a prefix.
  7. Fill the CYK table for a * a * a using the CNF grammar of §6. How many split points does the top cell try, which ones succeed, and what does the answer say about associativity?
    solution The filled table, rows by span length:
    lenfrom 1: afrom 2: *from 3: afrom 4: *from 5: a
    5E, T
    4T₁∅
    3E, T∅E, T
    2T₁∅T₁∅
    1E, T, FME, T, FME, T, F
    Length 1: positions 1, 3, 5 (the as) get \(\{E,T,F\}\); positions 2 and 4 (the *s) get \(\{M\}\). Length 2: (1,2) and (3,4) get \(\{T_1\}\) by \(T_1 \to T\,M\); (2,3) and (4,5) are empty, since no production begins with \(M\). Length 3: (1,3) gets \(\{E,T\}\) from split \(k = 2\), pairing \(T_1\) with \(F \in \mathrm{tab}[3][3]\) via \(E \to T_1 F\) and \(T \to T_1 F\); (3,5) gets \(\{E,T\}\) the same way from \(k = 4\). Length 4: (1,4) tries \(k = 1,2,3\), pairing \((\{E,T,F\}, \emptyset)\), then \((\{T_1\}, \{E,T\})\), then \((\{E,T\}, \{M\})\). The first has an empty partner; the second fails because \(E \to T_1 F\) and \(T \to T_1 F\) both want an \(F\) on the right and \(\mathrm{tab}[3][4] = \{E,T\}\) has none; the third succeeds, since \(T \in \mathrm{tab}[1][3]\) and \(M \in \mathrm{tab}[4][4]\) match \(T_1 \to T\,M\). So \(\mathrm{tab}[1][4] = \{T_1\}\), and (2,5) stays empty. Length 5: four split points. \(k = 1\) and \(k = 3\) each pair something with an empty cell; \(k = 2\) pairs \(T_1\) with \(\mathrm{tab}[3][5] = \{E,T\}\) and fails for want of an \(F\); only \(k = 4\) works, pairing \(T_1 \in \mathrm{tab}[1][4]\) with \(F \in \mathrm{tab}[5][5]\) via \(E \to T_1 F\) and \(T \to T_1 F\). The top cell is \(\{E,T\}\), which contains the start symbol: accepted. The single successful split at \(k = 4\) puts the last * at the root, with a * a beneath it on the left: left-associative, exactly as the left-recursive rule \(T \to T * F\) requires. Had the grammar been right-recursive, the successful split would have been at \(k = 2\) instead.
  8. A colleague proposes replacing CYK with recursive descent everywhere, “since \(\Theta(n)\) beats \(\Theta(n^{3})\)”. Give three concrete situations in which that replacement is not available, and one in which CYK is not available either.
    solution (1) Left recursion in the grammar as given. Naive descent does not terminate on \(E \to E + T\); it must be transformed first, and for grammars where the left recursion is indirect (\(A \Rightarrow^{*} A\alpha\) through several nonterminals) that transformation is mechanical but disfiguring. CYK does not care. (2) Ambiguity that must be preserved. Natural-language parsers want all the trees, ranked by a probabilistic model; recursive descent commits to one path per choice point and structurally cannot enumerate them. CYK's table represents them all at once. (3) Grammars that are not LL(k) for any k. Whether a single production applies may depend on arbitrarily distant input, so no bounded lookahead decides it and descent degrades to backtracking, which is exponential in the worst case; CYK is \(\Theta(n^{3})\) on every input, adversarial or not. Where CYK is unavailable: streaming or interactive input. CYK needs the entire string in hand before it can start (its first row is indexed by position, and cells depend on later positions), and it needs \(\Theta(n^{2})\) space, so it cannot parse an unbounded stream or report an error at the first offending character. Recursive descent reads left to right, uses \(O(d)\) space, and stops at the first token that cannot continue any parse: that is why editors and compilers, which need incremental behaviour and good error positions, use top-down or LR parsers rather than CYK.
