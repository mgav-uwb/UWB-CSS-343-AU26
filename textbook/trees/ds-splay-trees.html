Splay Trees

Data Structures & Algorithms. Data structure.
A splay tree is a binary search tree that stores no balance information at all and rebalances itself as a side effect of being used: every access rotates the touched node to the root by a specific sequence of double rotations. The three step cases (zig, zig-zig, zig-zag) are given individually with before/after trees, then combined in a fully unrolled splay of a node at depth 5. Search, insert and delete are each one or two splays plus \(O(1)\) pointer work. The cost argument is amortized: the sum-of-log-subtree-sizes potential gives \(3\log_2 n + 1\) amortized per operation, while a single operation can still cost \(\Theta(n)\). Closes with the working-set and scanning behavior that makes splay trees act like a self-tuning cache, a comparison against AVL and red-black trees, and exercises with solutions.

A plain binary search tree degrades to a list on sorted input. The AVL tree and the red-black tree answer that by storing balance information in every node and repairing it after every update. The splay tree, due to Sleator and Tarjan (1985), answers it differently: store nothing, check nothing, and instead restructure on every access, moving the node just touched to the root along a path that halves the depth of everything beneath it. Individual operations can be slow; any long enough sequence of them cannot be.

1. Self-adjusting search trees

The balanced trees answer the question “how do I stop the tree from ever getting tall?” They answer it with an invariant (AVL: sibling heights differ by at most one; red-black: equal black height), per-node state to check that invariant, and repair code that runs after every update. The state costs memory in every node, and the repair code runs whether or not the access pattern would ever have caused trouble.

Splay trees ask a different question: “how do I stop a sequence of operations from being expensive?” A single operation is allowed to cost \(\Theta(n)\). What is guaranteed is that \(m\) operations on a tree of at most \(n\) keys cost \(O((m+n)\log n)\) in total, so the cost per operation, averaged over the sequence, is \(O(\log n)\). This is an amortized guarantee in the exact sense of the Amortized Analysis handout: no probability, no assumption about the input, just an accounting identity over the whole run.

Definition (splay tree) A splay tree is a binary search tree with no auxiliary balance data, in which every operation that touches a node \(x\) finishes by splaying \(x\): repeatedly applying a constant-time restructuring step at \(x\) until \(x\) is the root. The BST ordering invariant (all keys in the left subtree less than the node, all keys in the right subtree greater) is the only invariant the structure maintains.

The mechanism is rotation, the same order-preserving \(O(1)\) pointer move used by the AVL Trees and Treaps handouts. Rotating \(x\) above its parent lifts \(x\) one level and pushes the parent down one level, leaving the in-order key sequence unchanged. What is new here is not the rotation but which rotations, in which order: naively rotating \(x\) up one level at a time is not good enough, and the whole content of the structure is the specific pairing rule of §2.

2. The splay operation: three step cases

Let \(x\) be the node to move to the root, \(p\) its parent, and \(g\) its grandparent. Splaying repeats a single step until \(x\) is the root. Exactly one of three cases applies at each step, decided by whether \(g\) exists and, if it does, whether \(x\) and \(p\) are children on the same side or on opposite sides:

casewhen it applieswhat it doesrotations
zig\(p\) is the root (no \(g\))one rotation of \(x\) above \(p\)1
zig-zig\(x,p\) are both left children, or both right childrenrotate \(p\) above \(g\) first, then \(x\) above \(p\)2
zig-zag\(x\) is a left child and \(p\) a right child, or the reverserotate \(x\) above \(p\), then \(x\) above \(g\)2

Each case, and its mirror image, is drawn below with the four hanging subtrees named \(A, B, C, D\) so that the in-order sequence can be read off and compared. Since the zig case has only two nodes it has only three subtrees, \(A, B, C\).

Case 1: zig (\(x\) is a child of the root)

This is a single rotation, and it happens at most once per splay: only when \(x\) starts at an odd depth does it run, and then only as the very last step.

      /   \        zig at x       /   \
     x     C       ------>       A     p
   /   \                              /   \
  A     B                            B     C

in-order before:  A x B p C          in-order after:  A x B p C

The mirror image (\(x\) the right child of the root) is the same picture reflected: \(p(A, x(B,C))\) becomes \(x(p(A,B), C)\).

Case 2: zig-zig (\(x\) and \(p\) on the same side)

This is the case that makes splay trees work, and the order of the two rotations is the point. The grandparent is rotated first: \(p\) goes above \(g\), and only then does \(x\) go above \(p\). The result turns a three-node chain into a chain of the other handedness with \(x\) on top, which is what folds a long path in half rather than merely reversing it.

Note where the depths land. \(A\) rises from depth 3 to depth 1 and \(B\) from 3 to 2, while \(C\) drops from 2 to 3 and \(D\) from 1 to 3. The deep side of the chain, the side the search path came down, is the side that gains, and it gains by roughly halving: this is the step that folds a long path instead of merely rotating its top off.

Case 3: zig-zag (\(x\) and \(p\) on opposite sides)

Here \(x\) is rotated twice, first above \(p\) and then above \(g\). The two rotations point in opposite directions, and the effect is to pull \(x\) up between the two: \(p\) and \(g\) become its children.

      /   \                             /   \

A zig-zag is exactly the AVL “double rotation” applied to a left-right (or right-left) configuration. It is perfectly balancing locally: all four subtrees end at the same depth 2.

Why not just rotate \(x\) up one level at a time? The obvious algorithm, move-to-root, applies single rotations all the way up, ignoring the grandparent. It also brings \(x\) to the root and it also preserves order, but it does not give the amortized bound, and the reason is visible on a path. Take the right path \(1\to2\to\cdots\to8\) (height \(7\)) and pull \(8\) to the root: 
splay(8):  zig-zig, zig-zig, zig-zig, zig     move-to-root(8): 7 single rotations
       8                                              8
     /                                              /
     1                                              1
      \                                              \
       3                                              2
     /   \                                             \
     2     5                                            3
         /   \                                           \
         4     7                                          4
             /                                             \
             6                                              5
                                                             \
                                                              6
                                                               \
                                                                7

  height 5                                      height 7, still a path
Splaying folded the path: the seven remaining nodes ended at depths \(1,2,3,3,4,4,5\) instead of \(1,2,3,4,5,6,7\). Move-to-root merely moved \(8\) off the top of an otherwise untouched path, so the next access to the deepest key costs just as much again. Repeating that pattern gives \(\Theta(n)\) per access forever, which is why the grandparent-first pairing in zig-zig is not a stylistic choice. 
// Splay tree core: node, rotation, and the splay loop. No balance field:
// the node carries a key, two children, and a parent pointer, nothing else.
struct Node {
    int key;
    Node *left = nullptr, *right = nullptr, *parent = nullptr;
    explicit Node(int k) : key(k) {}
};

static void attachLeft (Node* x, Node* c) { x->left  = c; if (c) c->parent = x; }
static void attachRight(Node* x, Node* c) { x->right = c; if (c) c->parent = x; }

// Rotate x above its parent. In-order is unchanged; x rises one level.
static void rotate(Node* x) {
    Node* p = x->parent;
    Node* g = p->parent;
    if (p->left == x) { attachLeft (p, x->right); attachRight(x, p); }
    else              { attachRight(p, x->left ); attachLeft (x, p); }
    x->parent = g;
    if (g) { if (g->left == p) g->left = x; else g->right = x; }
}

// Move x to the root, two levels at a time where possible.
Node* splay(Node* x) {
    while (x->parent) {
        Node* p = x->parent;
        Node* g = p->parent;
        if (!g) {                                   // ZIG
            rotate(x);
        } else if ((g->left == p) == (p->left == x)) {  // ZIG-ZIG: same side
            rotate(p);                              // grandparent first!
            rotate(x);
        } else {                                    // ZIG-ZAG: opposite sides
            rotate(x);
            rotate(x);
        }
    }
    return x;                                       // x is now the root
}

class Node:
    __slots__ = ("key", "left", "right", "parent")
    def __init__(self, key):
        self.key = key
        self.left = self.right = self.parent = None

def attach_left(x, c):
    x.left = c
    if c: c.parent = x

def attach_right(x, c):
    x.right = c
    if c: c.parent = x

def rotate(x):                       # x rises one level; in-order unchanged
    p = x.parent
    g = p.parent
    if p.left is x:
        attach_left(p, x.right); attach_right(x, p)
    else:
        attach_right(p, x.left);  attach_left(x, p)
    x.parent = g
    if g:
        if g.left is p: g.left = x
        else:           g.right = x

def splay(x):                        # move x to the root, two levels at a time
    while x.parent is not None:
        p = x.parent
        g = p.parent
        if g is None:                                  # ZIG
            rotate(x)
        elif (g.left is p) == (p.left is x):           # ZIG-ZIG: same side
            rotate(p)                                  # grandparent first!
            rotate(x)
        else:                                          # ZIG-ZAG: opposite sides
            rotate(x)
            rotate(x)

// Splay tree core in an arena: nodes live in a Vec and links are indices, so
// the parent pointers cost no borrow-checker gymnastics. No balance field.
type Ix = usize;
const NIL: Ix = usize::MAX;

struct Node { key: i32, left: Ix, right: Ix, parent: Ix }
struct Tree { a: Vec<Node>, root: Ix }

impl Tree {
    fn new_node(&mut self, key: i32) -> Ix {
        self.a.push(Node { key, left: NIL, right: NIL, parent: NIL });
        self.a.len() - 1
    }
    fn set_left(&mut self, x: Ix, c: Ix) {
        self.a[x].left = c;
        if c != NIL { self.a[c].parent = x; }
    }
    fn set_right(&mut self, x: Ix, c: Ix) {
        self.a[x].right = c;
        if c != NIL { self.a[c].parent = x; }
    }

    // Rotate x above its parent. In-order is unchanged; x rises one level.
    fn rotate(&mut self, x: Ix) {
        let p = self.a[x].parent;
        let g = self.a[p].parent;
        if self.a[p].left == x {
            let b = self.a[x].right; self.set_left(p, b);  self.set_right(x, p);
        } else {
            let b = self.a[x].left;  self.set_right(p, b); self.set_left(x, p);
        }
        self.a[x].parent = g;
        if g != NIL {
            if self.a[g].left == p { self.a[g].left = x; } else { self.a[g].right = x; }
        }
    }

    // Move x to the root, two levels at a time where possible.
    fn splay(&mut self, x: Ix) {
        while self.a[x].parent != NIL {
            let p = self.a[x].parent;
            let g = self.a[p].parent;
            if g == NIL {                                     // ZIG
                self.rotate(x);
            } else if (self.a[g].left == p) == (self.a[p].left == x) {
                self.rotate(p);                               // ZIG-ZIG, g first!
                self.rotate(x);
            } else {                                          // ZIG-ZAG
                self.rotate(x);
                self.rotate(x);
            }
        }
        self.root = x;
    }
}

The loop condition is x.parent != null, so the number of steps is \(\lceil d/2 \rceil\) where \(d\) is the starting depth of \(x\): the double steps consume two levels each and a single zig consumes the last one when \(d\) is odd. Each step is \(O(1)\), so one splay costs \(\Theta(d)\), the same order as the search that found \(x\).

3. A full splay, unrolled

Take the binary search tree on the thirteen keys \(10, 20, \dots, 130\) shown below, and splay the key \(40\), which sits at depth \(5\). The access path from the root is \(120 \to 100 \to 80 \to 20 \to 60 \to 40\), taking the directions left, left, left, right, left. Splaying reads that path from the bottom in pairs, so it will fire one of each case.

4. Search, insert and delete

Every operation is “do the plain BST thing, then splay something.” That last clause is not optional: without it the amortized bound is lost, because the tree never repays the cost of a deep access.

Search

Descend by key comparisons as in a plain BST. If the key is found, splay that node. If the key is not found, splay the last node visited (the leaf-ward end of the search path, which is the predecessor or successor of the missing key). Splaying on failure matters: an unsuccessful search still paid for a deep descent, and it must still restructure to pay it back.

Trace: an unsuccessful search for 45 In the §3 tree, searching for \(45\) walks \(120 \to 100 \to 80 \to 20 \to 60 \to 40 \to 50\) (at \(40\) we go right because \(45 \gt 40\); at \(50\) we would go left, but \(50\) has no left child, so the search fails there). The last node visited is \(50\), at depth \(6\), so we splay \(50\). Its path from the root is left, left, left, right, left, right, so the cases from the bottom are zig-zag \((50,40,60)\), zig-zag \((50,20,80)\), zig-zig \((50,100,120)\):

Insert

Descend as in a plain BST and attach the new node as a leaf, then splay the new node to the root. (If the key is already present, splay the existing node and change nothing else.)

Delete

Splay the node to be deleted to the root. It now has a left subtree \(L\) (all keys smaller) and a right subtree \(R\) (all keys larger). Detach both and discard the root. If \(L\) is empty, \(R\) is the whole answer. Otherwise join them: splay the maximum of \(L\) to \(L\)’s root; being the maximum it has no right child, so \(R\) can simply be hung there.

The two-children case that needs a successor hunt in Hibbard deletion disappears: splaying creates the empty slot the join needs, rather than searching for a node to move into it.

// Search, insert, delete. Each is plain BST work followed by a splay.
// `root` is passed and returned because every operation moves it.

// Descend to key, or to the last node on its search path; splay that node.
// Returns the new root; *found reports whether the key was present.
Node* search(Node* root, int key, bool* found) {
    if (!root) { *found = false; return nullptr; }
    Node* cur = root; Node* last = root;
    while (cur) {
        last = cur;
        if (key == cur->key) break;
        cur = (key < cur->key) ? cur->left : cur->right;
    }
    *found = (last->key == key);
    return splay(last);                 // splay even on failure
}

Node* insert(Node* root, int key) {
    if (!root) return new Node(key);
    Node* cur = root; Node* par = nullptr;
    while (cur) {
        par = cur;
        if (key == cur->key) return splay(cur);   // already present
        cur = (key < cur->key) ? cur->left : cur->right;
    }
    Node* n = new Node(key);            // attach as a leaf, then lift it
    if (key < par->key) attachLeft(par, n); else attachRight(par, n);
    return splay(n);
}

Node* remove(Node* root, int key) {
    bool found; root = search(root, key, &found);
    if (!found) return root;            // absent: the splay already happened
    Node* L = root->left; Node* R = root->right;
    if (L) L->parent = nullptr;
    if (R) R->parent = nullptr;
    delete root;
    if (!L) return R;
    Node* m = L; while (m->right) m = m->right;  // max of L
    L = splay(m);                       // now L->right is null
    attachRight(L, R);
    return L;
}

// `root` is passed and returned because every operation moves it.

static boolean lastFound;               // set by search()

// Descend to key, or to the last node on its search path; splay that node.
static Node search(Node root, int key) {
    if (root == null) { lastFound = false; return null; }
    Node cur = root, last = root;
    while (cur != null) {
        last = cur;
        if (key == cur.key) break;
        cur = (key < cur.key) ? cur.left : cur.right;
    }
    lastFound = (last.key == key);
    return splay(last);                 // splay even on failure
}

static Node insert(Node root, int key) {
    if (root == null) return new Node(key);
    Node cur = root, par = null;
    while (cur != null) {
        par = cur;
        if (key == cur.key) return splay(cur);    // already present
        cur = (key < cur.key) ? cur.left : cur.right;
    }
    Node n = new Node(key);             // attach as a leaf, then lift it
    if (key < par.key) attachLeft(par, n); else attachRight(par, n);
    return splay(n);
}

static Node remove(Node root, int key) {
    root = search(root, key);
    if (!lastFound) return root;        // absent: the splay already happened
    Node L = root.left, R = root.right;
    if (L != null) L.parent = null;
    if (R != null) R.parent = null;
    if (L == null) return R;
    Node m = L; while (m.right != null) m = m.right;   // max of L
    L = splay(m);                       // now L.right is null
    attachRight(L, R);
    return L;
}

def search(root, key):
    """Return (new_root, found). Splays the last node on the search path."""
    if root is None:
        return None, False
    cur = last = root
    while cur is not None:
        last = cur
        if key == cur.key: break
        cur = cur.left if key < cur.key else cur.right
    return splay(last), last.key == key      # splay even on failure

def insert(root, key):
    if root is None:
        return Node(key)
    cur, par = root, None
    while cur is not None:
        par = cur
        if key == cur.key:
            return splay(cur)                # already present
        cur = cur.left if key < cur.key else cur.right
    n = Node(key)                            # attach as a leaf, then lift it
    if key < par.key: attach_left(par, n)
    else:             attach_right(par, n)
    return splay(n)

def delete(root, key):
    root, found = search(root, key)
    if not found:
        return root                          # the splay already happened
    L, R = root.left, root.right
    if L: L.parent = None
    if R: R.parent = None
    if L is None:
        return R
    m = L
    while m.right: m = m.right               # max of L
    L = splay(m)                             # now L.right is None
    attach_right(L, R)
    return L
// Search, insert, delete on the arena tree. Each is plain BST work
// followed by a splay; self.root is updated by splay().
impl Tree {
    // Descend to key, or to the last node on its search path; splay it.
    fn search(&mut self, key: i32) -> bool {
        if self.root == NIL { return false; }
        let (mut cur, mut last) = (self.root, self.root);
        while cur != NIL {
            last = cur;
            if key == self.a[cur].key { break; }
            cur = if key < self.a[cur].key { self.a[cur].left } else { self.a[cur].right };
        }
        self.splay(last);                      // splay even on failure
        self.a[last].key == key
    }

    fn insert(&mut self, key: i32) {
        if self.root == NIL { self.root = self.new_node(key); return; }
        let (mut cur, mut par) = (self.root, NIL);
        while cur != NIL {
            par = cur;
            if key == self.a[cur].key { self.splay(cur); return; }
            cur = if key < self.a[cur].key { self.a[cur].left } else { self.a[cur].right };
        }
        let n = self.new_node(key);            // attach as a leaf, then lift it
        if key < self.a[par].key { self.set_left(par, n) } else { self.set_right(par, n) }
        self.splay(n);
    }

    fn remove(&mut self, key: i32) {
        if !self.search(key) { return; }       // absent: splay already happened
        let r = self.root;
        let (l, rr) = (self.a[r].left, self.a[r].right);
        if l  != NIL { self.a[l ].parent = NIL; }
        if rr != NIL { self.a[rr].parent = NIL; }
        if l == NIL { self.root = rr; return; }
        self.root = l;
        let mut m = l;                          // max of L
        while self.a[m].right != NIL { m = self.a[m].right; }
        self.splay(m);                          // now m has no right child
        self.set_right(m, rr);
        self.root = m;
    }
}

Every operation costs \(\Theta(d)\) where \(d\) is the depth reached: the descent is \(d\) comparisons and the splay is \(\lceil d/2\rceil\) constant-time steps. Deletion is two splays, so at most twice that. Nothing here bounds \(d\); the bound comes from §6, and it is a bound on the sum over a sequence, not on any one \(d\).

5. Why no balance field is stored

The balanced trees store data whose only purpose is to answer “is the invariant still true here?”: an AVL node keeps a height or a two-bit balance factor, a red-black node keeps a colour bit, a treap node keeps a random priority. A splay node keeps a key, two child pointers, and (for the bottom-up formulation above) a parent pointer. There is nothing to initialise, nothing to recompute after a rotation, and nothing that can be corrupted into an inconsistent state.

Three consequences follow.

  • Space. An AVL node needs an extra integer field (or, packed, two bits that in practice still cost a whole word of alignment). Over \(n\) nodes that is real memory. A splay node has no such field at all. The parent pointer used above is a convenience, not a requirement: the top-down formulation of splaying builds the two halves of the result as it descends and needs no parent pointers, giving a node identical in size to a plain BST node.
  • Code size and case count. There is one rebalancing routine, splay, with three cases, and it is the same routine for search, insert and delete. Red-black insertion plus deletion is a noticeably larger body of case analysis, and every case also has to fix up the stored colours.
  • No invariant to violate. Correctness of a splay tree rests only on the BST ordering property, which rotations preserve by construction. Every intermediate tree in §3 is a legal splay tree. By contrast, an AVL tree in the middle of a fix-up is temporarily not an AVL tree, and code that observes it there sees a broken structure.

The price is paid in the guarantee. Because no invariant is enforced, nothing prevents a splay tree from being a path. Insert \(1,2,\dots,n\) in ascending order into an initially empty splay tree: each new key is larger than everything present, so it attaches as the rightmost leaf and is then splayed to the root with the whole tree hanging to its left. The result for \(n = 8\) is a left path of height \(7\):

Searching for \(1\) in that tree touches all \(8\) nodes. That is the worst case and it is genuinely \(\Theta(n)\): a splay tree makes no promise about any single operation. What §6 shows is that the next operations cannot all be that bad, because this one just paid for a large amount of restructuring.

Why writes on every read can be a problem A splay tree mutates on search. Two consequences are practical rather than asymptotic: a splay tree cannot be shared by concurrent readers without a writer’s lock (a red-black tree can), and a read-mostly workload on a splay tree dirties cache lines and, in a persistent or memory-mapped setting, dirties pages. This is the main reason standard libraries ship red-black trees rather than splay trees for their general-purpose ordered maps.

6. The amortized \(O(\log n)\) bound

The analysis is the potential method of the Amortized Analysis handout: choose a function \(\Phi\) from tree states to non-negative reals, and define the amortized cost of an operation as its actual cost plus the change in potential, \(\hat{c} = c + \Phi_{\text{after}} - \Phi_{\text{before}}\). Summing over a sequence telescopes: \(\sum c_i = \sum \hat{c}_i + \Phi_0 - \Phi_m \le \sum \hat{c}_i + \Phi_0\) whenever \(\Phi \ge 0\). So a bound on every \(\hat{c}_i\) plus a bound on \(\Phi_0\) bounds the total actual cost.

Definition (size, rank, potential) For a node \(x\), let \(s(x)\) be the number of nodes in the subtree rooted at \(x\) (counting \(x\)), and let the rank be \(r(x) = \log_2 s(x)\). The potential of a tree \(T\) is \[ \Phi(T) \;=\; \sum_{x \in T} r(x) \;=\; \sum_{x \in T} \log_2 s(x). \] Every \(s(x) \ge 1\), so every \(r(x) \ge 0\) and \(\Phi \ge 0\); and every \(s(x) \le n\), so \(\Phi \le n\log_2 n\).

Read \(\Phi\) as “how lopsided the tree is.” A path of \(n\) nodes has subtree sizes \(1,2,\dots,n\), giving \(\Phi = \sum_{k=1}^n \log_2 k = \log_2(n!) \approx n\log_2 n - 1.44n\), near the maximum. A perfectly balanced tree has most subtrees small and \(\Phi\) close to \(n\). Deep trees are stored-up work; splaying spends that store.

Everything rests on one inequality, which is where the specific shapes of §2 enter the algebra.

Lemma (concavity of \(\log\)) For any \(a, b > 0\), \(\;\log_2 a + \log_2 b \le 2\log_2\!\left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right) = 2\log_2(a+b) - 2.\)
Proof. By the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality, \(\sqrt{ab} \le (a+b)/2\). Both sides are positive, and \(\log_2\) is increasing, so \(\tfrac12(\log_2 a + \log_2 b) = \log_2\sqrt{ab} \le \log_2\frac{a+b}{2}\). Double both sides. ∎

Write \(r\) for ranks before a step and \(r'\) for ranks after it. The next lemma bounds one splay step; the theorem then telescopes over the steps of a whole splay.

Lemma (one splay step) Let a splay step move \(x\) up. Its amortized cost (rotations performed, plus \(\Delta\Phi\)) satisfies \[ \hat{c}_{\text{step}} \;\le\; 3\bigl(r'(x) - r(x)\bigr) + [\,\text{step is a zig}\,], \] where the bracket is \(1\) for a zig and \(0\) for a zig-zig or zig-zag.
Proof. Only \(x\), \(p\) and \(g\) change subtree size, so \(\Delta\Phi\) involves only their ranks.

zig-zig. Actual cost \(2\). Since \(x\) ends where \(g\) began, \(r'(x) = r(g)\), so \[ \hat{c} = 2 + \bigl(r'(x)+r'(p)+r'(g)\bigr) - \bigl(r(x)+r(p)+r(g)\bigr) = 2 + r'(p) + r'(g) - r(x) - r(p). \] After the step \(p\) is a child of \(x\), so \(r'(p) \le r'(x)\); before it \(x\) was a child of \(p\), so \(r(p) \ge r(x)\). Hence \(\hat{c} \le 2 + r'(x) + r'(g) - 2r(x)\). It therefore suffices to show \(2 + r'(g) + r(x) \le 2r'(x)\), because then \(\hat{c} \le 2 + r'(x) + r'(g) - 2r(x) \le 3r'(x) - 3r(x)\). Look at the §2 picture: after the step, \(x\)’s subtree contains \(x\) itself, \(x\)’s old subtree (\(A, B\) and \(x\)) and \(g\)’s new subtree (\(C, D\) and \(g\)), which are disjoint, so \(s(x) + s'(g) \le s'(x)\). By the concavity lemma with \(a = s(x)\), \(b = s'(g)\), \[ r(x) + r'(g) \;\le\; 2\log_2\bigl(s(x)+s'(g)\bigr) - 2 \;\le\; 2\log_2 s'(x) - 2 \;=\; 2r'(x) - 2, \] which is the required inequality.

zig-zag. Actual cost \(2\), and again \(r'(x) = r(g)\), so \(\hat{c} = 2 + r'(p) + r'(g) - r(x) - r(p) \le 2 + r'(p) + r'(g) - 2r(x)\) using \(r(p) \ge r(x)\). After the step \(p\) and \(g\) are the two children of \(x\), so their subtrees are disjoint and \(s'(p) + s'(g) \le s'(x)\); the concavity lemma gives \(r'(p) + r'(g) \le 2r'(x) - 2\). Hence \(\hat{c} \le 2\bigl(r'(x) - r(x)\bigr) \le 3\bigl(r'(x)-r(x)\bigr)\), the last step because \(r'(x) \ge r(x)\).

zig. Actual cost \(1\). Only \(x\) and \(p\) change, and after the step \(p\) is a child of \(x\) so \(r'(p) \le r(p)\). Thus \(\hat{c} = 1 + r'(x) + r'(p) - r(x) - r(p) \le 1 + r'(x) - r(x) \le 1 + 3\bigl(r'(x) - r(x)\bigr)\). ∎
Theorem (access lemma) Splaying a node \(x\) in a tree with root \(t\) has amortized cost at most \(3\bigl(r(t) - r(x)\bigr) + 1 \le 3\log_2 n + 1\), where the ranks are taken before the splay.
Proof. Let the splay consist of steps \(1, \dots, k\), and let \(r_i(x)\) be \(x\)’s rank after step \(i\), with \(r_0(x) = r(x)\) and \(r_k(x) = r(t)\) (after the last step \(x\) is the root, so its subtree is the whole tree). Summing the step lemma, \[ \sum_{i=1}^{k} \hat{c}_i \;\le\; \sum_{i=1}^{k} 3\bigl(r_i(x)-r_{i-1}(x)\bigr) + 1 \;=\; 3\bigl(r_k(x) - r_0(x)\bigr) + 1 \;=\; 3\bigl(r(t)-r(x)\bigr)+1, \] the sum telescoping and the \(+1\) appearing at most once because at most one step of a splay is a zig (a zig requires \(x\) to be a child of the root, which happens only at the end). Finally \(r(t) = \log_2 n\) and \(r(x) \ge 0\). ∎
Corollary (sequence cost) A sequence of \(m\) splay-tree operations on a tree that never holds more than \(n\) keys costs \(O\bigl((m+n)\log n\bigr)\) time in total, that is \(O(\log n)\) amortized per operation.
Proof. An operation performs at most two splays plus \(O(1)\) other work. Its actual cost is proportional to the depth \(d\) it descends to, and the splay that follows performs exactly \(d\) rotations, so the operation’s actual cost is at most a constant times the actual cost the access lemma charges, plus \(O(1)\). Its amortized cost is therefore at most \(c \cdot 2(3\log_2 n + 1) + O(1) = O(\log n)\). Telescoping the potential over the sequence, \(\sum_i c_i = \sum_i \hat{c}_i + \Phi_0 - \Phi_m\). Now \(\Phi_m \ge 0\) and \(\Phi_0 \le n\log_2 n\), so \(\sum_i c_i \le m \cdot O(\log n) + n\log_2 n = O((m+n)\log n)\). ∎

The bookkeeping on the §3 splay

The potential is not a metaphor; it is arithmetic on the actual tree. For the thirteen-node tree of §3 the subtree sizes are \(s(120)=13,\; s(100)=11,\; s(80)=9,\; s(20)=7,\; s(60)=5,\; s(40)=3\) along the access path and \(1\) at each of the seven leaves, with \(s(90)=s(110)=s(130)=1\). Summing \(\log_2 s(x)\) gives \(\Phi_{\text{before}} = 17.044\). After the splay the sizes are \(s(40)=13,\; s(120)=9,\; s(80)=7,\; s(20)=3,\; s(60)=3,\; s(100)=3\) and \(1\) elsewhere, giving \(\Phi_{\text{after}} = 14.433\).

Where the constant 3 comes from The \(3\) is not tight; it is the smallest constant that makes all three cases fit one formula. The zig-zag case alone gives \(2(r'(x)-r(x))\), and the zig-zig case is the one that needs \(3\). Tighter bounds are known for restricted access patterns: the sequential-access result of §7 has a small constant and no \(\log n\) factor at all.

7. Working sets and caching behavior

The \(O(\log n)\) amortized bound is the floor, not the ceiling of what splaying gives. Because the structure reshapes itself around what is actually accessed, its cost adapts to the access pattern in ways a fixed-shape balanced tree cannot. Three results make this precise; all three follow from the same potential technique with a weighted rank \(r(x) = \log_2\bigl(\sum_{y \in \text{subtree}(x)} w(y)\bigr)\) in place of the unweighted one.

Theorem (static optimality, Sleator-Tarjan) Suppose key \(i\) is accessed \(q_i \ge 1\) times in a sequence of \(m\) accesses. The total time a splay tree spends is \(O\bigl(m + \sum_i q_i \log_2 (m/q_i)\bigr)\).

The quantity \(\sum_i q_i \log_2(m/q_i)\) is \(m\) times the empirical entropy of the access distribution, and it is exactly (to within a constant factor) the cost of the best possible fixed binary search tree for that distribution: the optimal BST that one would build knowing all the frequencies in advance. A splay tree matches it without being told the frequencies, and without storing any of them.

Theorem (working set, Sleator-Tarjan) Let \(t_j\) be the number of distinct keys accessed since the previous access to the key accessed at time \(j\) (or \(n\) if this is its first access). The total time for \(m\) accesses is \(O\bigl(n\log n + m + \sum_{j=1}^{m} \log_2 (t_j+2)\bigr)\).

This is the caching statement. The cost of an access depends on how many other keys have been touched since that key was last used, not on \(n\). A key re-accessed after \(t\) distinct others costs \(O(\log t)\), so a program working inside a small hot set pays logarithmically in the size of the hot set, whatever the size of the tree. This is a least-recently-used cache behavior obtained without any cache: the splaying is the eviction policy, in that a key not touched for a long time sinks as other keys are pulled over it.

Dynamic optimality Sleator and Tarjan conjectured something stronger than any of the above: that a splay tree is within a constant factor of the cost of the best possible binary search tree algorithm for the given access sequence, even one that knows the whole sequence in advance and may rotate at will. This is the dynamic optimality conjecture, and after four decades it is still open. The best known general result is that certain structures (Tango trees among them) are within \(O(\log\log n)\) of optimal; whether splay trees themselves are within \(O(1)\) is unknown.

This catalog’s balanced search trees differ in what kind of promise they make and what they spend to keep it.

Against the AVL tree: AVL guarantees \(h \le 1.44\log_2 n\) at all times and never exceeds \(O(\log n)\) on any single operation, which is what a hard real-time deadline needs. A splay tree can spend \(\Theta(n)\) on one operation and is therefore unusable under such a deadline, but it stores no height field, needs no rebalance case analysis, and beats AVL on skewed access patterns by the margin measured in §7. AVL is also the more aggressively balanced of the two on random access, so for a read-heavy uniform workload with no locality it will win on comparisons.

Against the red-black tree: red-black keeps a looser bound (\(2\log_2 n\)) than AVL in exchange for fewer restructuring operations per update, and it is the structure standard libraries ship precisely because it never mutates on a read and so supports concurrent readers and read-only pages. The splay tree occupies the opposite corner of the same trade: the least stored state of all these structures and the best adaptivity, at the cost of writing on every read and giving up the per-operation guarantee. Both are worth knowing as the two ends of the “how much do you pay to maintain an invariant?” spectrum.

Two other neighbours are worth naming. The treap also drops the explicit invariant, but replaces it with randomness rather than with restructuring-on-access, giving an expected rather than an amortized bound; a treap’s shape is independent of the access pattern, which is exactly the property splaying gives up in order to adapt. The skip list reaches the same expected \(O(\log n)\) with coin flips over a linked list instead of a tree. For external storage neither applies and the B-tree (with its in-memory ancestor the 2-3 tree) is the right shape, because there the cost model counts blocks, not comparisons. The amortized reasoning used here is developed on its own in the Amortized Analysis handout, alongside the other standard applications of the potential method, and the asymptotic vocabulary it uses is fixed in Asymptotic Notation.

9. Exercises (with solutions)

  1. Name the case that fires at each step of splaying \(x\), given that the path from the root down to \(x\) takes the directions right, right, left, right, left (so \(x\) is at depth 5). How many rotations does the splay perform?
    solution Splaying reads the path from the bottom in pairs. The last two directions are right then left, opposite sides: zig-zag. The previous pair is right then left again: zig-zag. One direction is left over at the top, so the last step is a zig. Three steps, \(2+2+1 = 5\) rotations. In general a node at depth \(d\) needs \(\lceil d/2 \rceil\) steps and exactly \(d\) rotations, since each rotation lifts \(x\) one level.
  2. Splay the key \(30\) in the tree \(50\bigl(20(10,\,40(30,\,45)),\;60\bigr)\). Give the case of each step and the final tree.
    solution \(30\) is at depth 3, reached by left, right, left. Bottom pair is right then left, opposite sides, so step 1 is a zig-zag on \((x,p,g) = (30, 40, 20)\): \(30\) rises between them, giving \(20(10,\cdot)\) and \(40(\cdot,45)\) as its children, so the subtree becomes \(30(20(10,\cdot),\,40(\cdot,45))\) hanging as \(50\)’s left child. Now \(30\) is a child of the root, so step 2 is a zig: \(30\) rises, handing its right subtree \(40(\cdot,45)\) to \(50\) as \(50\)’s left child. Final tree: \(30\bigl(20(10,\cdot),\;50(40(\cdot,45),\,60)\bigr)\). In-order is \(10,20,30,40,45,50,60\) before and after, the height is \(3\) both times, and \(30\) has moved from depth 3 to depth 0. Two steps, three rotations. (A splay does not have to shorten the tree; what it guarantees is that the accessed node reaches the root and that the potential does not rise by more than the access lemma allows.)
  3. A splay tree contains \(10, 20, \dots, 130\) in the shape of §3. You search for \(35\), which is absent. Which node gets splayed, and why does splaying on an unsuccessful search matter?
    solution The search walks \(120 \to 100 \to 80 \to 20 \to 60 \to 40 \to 30\) (at \(40\) we go left because \(35 \lt 40\), reaching \(30\); at \(30\) we would go right, but \(30\) has no right child, so the search fails). The last node visited is \(30\), at depth \(6\), and it is splayed. It matters because the amortized argument charges the descent, which cost 7 node visits, against the drop in potential that the splay produces. If the algorithm skipped the splay on failure, an adversary could repeat the same failed deep search forever at \(\Theta(n)\) each time, with no restructuring ever occurring and the potential never falling: the access lemma would not apply, because it bounds the cost of a splay, not of a bare descent.
  4. Show that a splay tree can be forced into a path of height \(n-1\), and that a single subsequent operation on it can cost \(\Theta(n)\). Reconcile this with the \(O(\log n)\) amortized bound.
    solution Insert \(1, 2, \dots, n\) in ascending order into an empty tree. Each new key exceeds every key present, so BST placement attaches it as the rightmost leaf and the splay lifts it to the root with the entire existing tree as its left child. Inductively the result is a left path \(n(n{-}1(\cdots(2(1))))\) of height \(n-1\), verified above for \(n=8\). Searching for \(1\) then visits all \(n\) nodes, so that operation costs \(\Theta(n)\). No contradiction arises: the amortized bound is a statement about a sum. The path has \(\Phi = \log_2(n!) \approx n\log_2 n - 1.44n\), close to the maximum possible, and the expensive search collapses it, releasing \(\Theta(n)\) units of potential which pay for the \(\Theta(n)\) rotations. The bookkeeping identity \(\sum c_i = \sum \hat{c}_i + \Phi_0 - \Phi_m\) permits any single \(c_i\) to be large so long as the potential paid for it, and the \(n\log_2 n\) term in the corollary is exactly the budget for such events.
  5. The splay step for the zig-zig case rotates the grandparent first. Suppose instead it rotated \(x\) twice (that is, treated zig-zig like zig-zag). Draw the result on the generic \(g(p(x(A,B),C),D)\) configuration and compare it with the correct zig-zig result. Then explain, using the path of §2, why the difference is asymptotic and not cosmetic.
    solution Rotating \(x\) above \(p\) gives \(g(x(A,\,p(B,C)),\,D)\); rotating \(x\) above \(g\) gives \(x\bigl(A,\;g(p(B,C),\,D)\bigr)\). The correct zig-zig result is \(x\bigl(A,\;p(B,\,g(C,D))\bigr)\). Both put \(x\) at the root and both preserve the in-order sequence \(A\,x\,B\,p\,C\,g\,D\), so on one triple the difference looks cosmetic. It is not, because the two behave oppositely when the configuration repeats down a long path. On the right path \(1 \to 2 \to \cdots \to 8\), splaying \(8\) with the correct zig-zig produces a tree of height \(5\) in which the remaining nodes sit at depths \(1,2,3,3,4,4,5\): the path was folded roughly in half. Applying single rotations only (which is what the wrong version degenerates to on a straight path) leaves \(8\) at the root above an untouched path \(1 \to 2 \to \cdots \to 7\) of height \(7\), so accessing the deepest key again costs \(\Theta(n)\), and repeating that access costs \(\Theta(n)\) every time. Only the grandparent-first order makes the halving happen, and the halving is what the concavity inequality \(s(x)+s'(g) \le s'(x)\) in the zig-zig proof records.
  6. Compute the potential \(\Phi = \sum_x \log_2 s(x)\) of the two trees \(T_1 = 30(20(10,\cdot),\,40(\cdot,50))\) and \(T_2 = 50(40(30(20(10,\cdot),\cdot),\cdot),\cdot)\), both on the five keys \(10,20,30,40,50\). Which is larger, and what does the difference mean operationally?
    solution \(T_1\): sizes are \(s(30)=5\), \(s(20)=2\), \(s(40)=2\), \(s(10)=s(50)=1\), so \(\Phi = \log_2 5 + 2\log_2 2 = 2.3219 + 2 = 4.3219\). \(T_2\) is a path: sizes \(5,4,3,2,1\), so \(\Phi = \log_2 5 + \log_2 4 + \log_2 3 + \log_2 2 = 2.3219 + 2 + 1.5850 + 1 = 6.9069\). The path has the larger potential, by \(2.585\). Operationally, \(T_2\) is carrying about \(2.6\) units of prepaid work: an access to its deepest key \(10\) will perform many rotations, but the potential released as the path collapses covers most of them, so the amortized charge stays small. Potential is stored-up imbalance, and a balanced tree has almost none to spend, which is consistent with its never needing to.
  7. Delete \(120\) from the final tree of §3, namely \(40\bigl(20(10,30),\;120(80(60(50,70),\,100(90,110)),\,130)\bigr)\). Give each splay and the resulting tree.
    solution Splay \(120\) first. It is the right child of the root \(40\), so one zig suffices: \(120\) rises, and its left subtree (rooted at \(80\)) becomes \(40\)’s right child. The tree is now \(120\bigl(40(20(10,30),\,80(60(50,70),\,100(90,110))),\,130\bigr)\). Detach: \(L = 40(20(10,30),\,80(60(50,70),100(90,110)))\) and \(R = 130\); discard the root. Splay the maximum of \(L\), which is \(110\), at depth 3 in \(L\) reached by right, right, right: the bottom pair is on the same side, so one zig-zig on \((x,p,g) = (110,100,80)\) lifts \(110\) to depth 1 and leaves \(100\bigl(80(60(50,70),\,90),\,\cdot\bigr)\) as its left subtree; then a zig on \((110,40)\) makes \(110\) the root of \(L\), handing \(40\) its old left part. \(L\) becomes \(110\bigl(40(20(10,30),\;100(80(60(50,70),90),\cdot)),\;\cdot\bigr)\), whose root has no right child, as the join requires. Hang \(R\) there: the final tree is \(110\bigl(40(20(10,30),\,100(80(60(50,70),90),\cdot)),\;130\bigr)\). In-order is \(10,20,\dots,110,130\): \(120\) is gone and everything else is in place. One rotation in the first splay, three in the second.
  8. A workload accesses \(10\) hot keys \(90\%\) of the time out of a tree of \(1024\) keys. Estimate the average access cost for a splay tree and for an AVL tree, and say which theorem justifies the splay estimate.
    solution An AVL tree pays \(\Theta(\log_2 1024) = \Theta(10)\) on every access, hot or cold, because its shape does not depend on the access frequencies. A splay tree pays roughly \(\log_2 10 \approx 3.3\) on a hot access (the hot keys stay near the root, since the number of distinct other keys touched between two hot accesses is small) and \(O(\log_2 1024)\) on a cold one, for an average near \(0.9 \times 3.3 + 0.1 \times 10 \approx 4.0\). The measurement in §7 gives \(4.95\) overall against \(12.52\) for a uniform pattern on the same tree. The estimate is a little low because the cold accesses cost more than \(10\), not less: pushing the hot keys up pushes everything else down, measured at \(14.96\). The working-set theorem is what justifies it: the cost of an access is \(O(\log(t+1))\) where \(t\) counts the distinct keys touched since that key was last accessed, which for a hot key is about the size of the hot set, not of the tree. Static optimality gives the same conclusion through the entropy of the distribution.
