Homework 8: Divide and Conquer; Dynamic Programming

Binary search was first published in 1946, but a version correct for every array size did not appear in print until 1962. In 2006 Joshua Bloch reported that Java's own library binary search had overflowed for years on huge arrays, because it computed the middle as (low + high) / 2. Knuth, The Art of Computer Programming, vol. 3, §6.2.1; Bloch, Google Research blog, 2006

Six functions: a merge that counts the inversions it removes, a mergesort that counts every inversion of an array, Lomuto's partition, quickselect with a random pivot, rod cutting when every cut has a cost, and the length of a longest common subsequence. The starter has a self-running test battery; you fill in six TODOs. Quiz 7 (Monday Nov 30) draws on these functions.

Tasks

TODOFunctionWhat it must do
1mergeMerge two adjacent sorted runs, ties to the left, and return the number of cross inversions: pairs with the larger key in the left run.
2mergesortCountSort by top-down mergesort and return the number of inversions the range held, in time proportional to n log₂ n.
3partitionLomuto's partition around the last key, producing exactly the lecture's arrangement.
4quickselectThe k-th smallest key, partitioning around a random pivot and continuing into one side only.
5rodCutThe best total for a rod when every cut costs c, by a bottom-up table, recording the first piece of a best cutting for every length.
6lcsLengthThe length of a longest common subsequence of two strings, by the lecture's table.

The full contract of each function, with examples, is in the comment above it in the starter. Every function is also tested on large inputs (a million keys, a rod of length 5,000, strings of 3,000 characters) with a time limit: write each one with the running time its contract states.

Each check prints [PASS] or [FAIL]. The unmodified starter compiles and fails most of them.
