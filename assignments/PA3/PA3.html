Programming Assignment 3: mydiff

The Unix diff program, released with the Fifth Edition of Unix in 1974, finds a longest common subsequence of the two files’ lines; its authors published the method in 1976. J. W. Hunt and M. D. McIlroy, “An Algorithm for Differential File Comparison,” Computing Science Technical Report 41, Bell Laboratories, 1976

Comparing two files is the longest-common-subsequence problem with lines in place of characters: the lines the files share, in order, are an LCS, and every other line was deleted from the first file or inserted into the second. The diff you read is the LCS table’s traceback. You build the tool, mydiff, on the DP from Lectures 16 to 18 and Homework 8:

./mydiff fileA fileB              unified diff, 3 context lines
./mydiff -u N fileA fileB         unified diff, N context lines
./mydiff --stat fileA fileB       counts only: two rolling rows, any size
./mydiff --word i j fileA fileB   changes within line i of A and line j of B

The command-line tool mydiff.cpp is given. You write the four functions behind it, declared in the given diff.h, in diff.cpp:

  1. lcsLines: the LCS length over the files’ lines, with two rolling rows, so O(min(n, m)) memory. This is what --stat runs, and it must handle the generated 30,000-line pair, whose full table would need about 3.6 GB.
  2. diffLines: the full edit script, one Edit per line, each marked ' ' (in both files), '-' (only in A) or '+' (only in B). Trim the common prefix and suffix first: they are common lines, and trimming them is what keeps real inputs small enough for the full table. Then build the LCS table over the rest and trace back from its last cell. Within a run of changes, deletions come before insertions. A traceback walks from the end, so it meets them in the opposite order: choose your tie-break accordingly.
  3. unifiedDiff: the script as hunks. Each run of changes, with up to context common lines before and after it, becomes a hunk headed @@ -start,count +start,count @@; two runs whose context lines would touch or overlap share one hunk. Compare your output with expected-unified.txt.
  4. markLine: the same DP one level down, over the characters of one line from each file. Deleted characters are wrapped [-…-] and inserted ones {+…+}, one bracket pair per run, so jumps against leaps gives [-jum-]{+lea+}ps.

The LCS table and its recurrence are Lecture 16 (Wed Nov 25). The traceback and the rolling rows are Lecture 18 (Wed Dec 2), after the design is due; the Traceback & Space-Saving chapter covers both now.

Part 1: the design (due Mon Nov 30, 10 points)

One page, PDF, graded on its own terms: you may change course while you build, and the report says what changed.

  1. Tables and memory. Which table each function builds, its dimensions and what a cell means. Why two rolling rows give the length but cannot give the traceback, and how trimming the prefix and suffix bounds the table diffLines builds.
  2. Recurrence and traceback. The recurrence with its base cases, and the tie-break your traceback uses, with the reason it puts deletions before insertions.
  3. Test plan. The cases your driver.cpp will check: at least an empty file, identical files, one-line files, files with no line in common, and context 0.
  4. Greedy fails. A pair of small files on which greedy matching (keep each line of A that occurs later in B, matched to its next occurrence) gives a diff with more changes than the minimum, and the minimal diff. Lecture 14 showed why a greedy rule needs a proof; this is a rule without one.

Your testing (driver.cpp)

A main() of your own that calls the four functions directly on the cases in your test plan and prints whether each result is right. Check an edit script the way the grader does: its ' ' and '-' lines must spell A, its ' ' and '+' lines must spell B, and it must have exactly n + m − 2·LCS changes.

Report (one page)

  • Prediction. Before you measure, the time and the memory of the full diff as functions of the line counts, and the ratio each should show when both files double.
  • Measurement. One table over the bench_* pairs (1000 to 8000 lines): time and peak memory of ./mydiff, with both doubling ratios, plus the --stat run on the 30,000-line pair. Peak memory: /usr/bin/time -v on the lab (“Maximum resident set size”), /usr/bin/time -l on macOS.
  • Explanation. A few sentences: where the ratios match the prediction, where they do not, and why.
  • Greedy, revisited. Your design’s counterexample run through your mydiff.
  • Design against build. What changed from the design, and why.
How it is graded We compile your diff.cpp against our own main(). No particular alignment is required: an edit script is checked by rebuilding both files from it and counting its changes, a unified diff by patching A into B, and a markLine by stripping it back to both lines. Any correct tie-break passes, except the two conventions above (deletions before insertions in a run; the sample’s output exactly as in expected-unified.txt). A diff.cpp that does not compile scores 0 for correctness. The final exam has questions on PA3: the design, and what a function returns on a given input.
