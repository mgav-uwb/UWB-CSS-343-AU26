You build one binary search tree class, BinTree, whose balancing policy is chosen when the tree is constructed:

  • Mode::VANILLA: a plain BST, no rebalancing (Lecture 4);
  • Mode::AVL: height-balanced with rotations (Lecture 5);
  • Mode::REDBLACK: the left-leaning red-black insert (Lecture 6), extra credit.

Then you run the same adversarial input through the modes and measure what each policy buys. The class carries its own counters (rotations(), colorFlips()), so the theory becomes numbers your code produces: sorted keys 1 to 1023 give height 1023 in a plain BST and 10 in an AVL tree, at a price of 1013 rotations.

The second half is an operation the lectures do not give you: mergeWith, which combines two trees into one. You get a contract (the correct union, a height bound, the other tree untouched) and design the algorithm yourself.

What to implement

Everything is in bintree.h and bintree.cpp. The public interface is fixed; add your own private recursive helpers. displayTree is given, as a debugging aid.

  1. Rule of Three and equality. Deep copy constructor, destructor, and a self-assignment-safe operator= (the mode and the counters copy too). operator== and != mean same keys and same structure; the mode is not compared, so the same keys inserted in VANILLA and AVL trees are usually !=.
  2. Queries: isEmpty, size, contains, treeHeight() (the number of nodes on the longest path; an empty tree has height 0).
  3. insert, per mode. A duplicate returns false in every mode. Then the mode's fix-up: nothing for VANILLA; for AVL, update heights on the way up and apply the four rotation cases. (Extra credit: REDBLACK, the three-if left-leaning insert with the root kept black. If you skip it, let REDBLACK behave like VANILLA.)
  4. remove, in VANILLA mode only: Hibbard deletion with the in-order successor, freeing every removed node. In the balanced modes it returns false and changes nothing.
  5. Counters: rotations() counts every single rotation (a double rotation counts 2); colorFlips() counts red-black flips. Increment them inside your helpers.
  6. toSortedArray and mergeWith, your own algorithm. The contract:
    • afterwards the tree holds the union of the two key sets (shared keys once); other is unchanged; merging a tree with itself is safe;
    • the result is a valid BST with height at most 2·⌈log2(n+1)⌉, and stays consistent for its mode, so that later inserts keep the mode's guarantee;
    • you set mergeAuxBytes() to the peak number of heap bytes your merge allocated (vectors, stacks, every new node). The instructor's benchmark checks it with a counting allocator; within a factor of 2 is accepted.
    How you meet the contract is the design exercise. Directions to consider: insert the other tree's keys one by one; flatten both trees to sorted arrays, merge, and rebuild; the same without materializing the arrays, using two iterator stacks; or split/join merging. Predict your algorithm's time and space before you measure.
Why "consistent for its mode" matters. Suppose you merge by flattening and rebuilding a perfectly balanced tree: an excellent way to meet the height bound. In AVL mode, every node's stored height must then equal its true subtree height. Leave it at 0 and the tree looks balanced, but the next insert reads the wrong heights and rotates the wrong way. So after a rebuild, recompute the heights in one bottom-up pass (or merge by a method that maintains them), and test a few inserts after a merge in your own driver.

If you skip the red-black extra credit, the REDBLACK lines of the output will differ from the expected output; every other line should match. The given driver is the floor, not the ceiling: write your own mydriver.cpp covering the awkward cases, such as empty trees, self-assignment, all the remove cases, merging with and into an empty tree, and merging a tree with itself.

The four benchmark workloads the instructor uses are published in starter/workloads/ (w1a, w1b, w2a, w2b, w3a, w3b, w4a, w4b; with their generator, gen_workloads.cpp): build each tree in AVL mode by inserting in file order, then merge b into a. W1 is two equal trees, W2 a tiny tree into a big one, W3 two trees with 50% shared keys, W4 two disjoint ranges.

Report (one page)

  1. The head-to-head: the table the driver prints (heights and rotations for sorted 1 to 1023 in each mode you implemented), and two or three sentences on what each policy bought and what it cost.
  2. Your merge: the algorithm in a few sentences, its predicted time and auxiliary space as functions of n and m, and your measured times and mergeAuxBytes() on at least two of the workloads. If prediction and measurement disagree, explain why.
  3. One sentence on the interface decision: AVL deletion is doable, yet remove is refused in the balanced modes. Defend it or attack it.
