Data Structures, Algorithms & Discrete Mathematics II · Autumn 2026

The course

The internal workings of algorithms and data structures, from mathematical principles to implementation in C++. It opens with a review of C++, recursion and counting, then analysis of algorithms: how to measure a program's time and memory and reason about them formally. That lens is applied to search trees and balanced trees, heaps and priority queues, hash tables, graphs and tries; to the design paradigms greedy, divide and conquer, and dynamic programming; and to the discrete mathematics underneath them (induction, counting, recurrences, graph theory). It closes with string matching, regular expressions and finite automata.

A 5-credit course is about 15 hours of work a week in all: plan on about 11 outside class.

Course text

The course has its own online textbook, one chapter per topic, with worked examples, exercises with solutions, and links to the interactive demos. It is the assigned reading, and nothing has to be bought.

Optional reading

Not assigned, for a second explanation or for going further:

  • Robert Sedgewick and Kevin Wayne, Algorithms, 4th ed.: the whole book is on its free booksite, with all of its code (in Java). Sedgewick's older Algorithms in C++ (3rd ed., Parts 1–4 and Part 5) covers much of the same ground in C++; it is in print, and the library has one copy.
  • Thomas Cormen, Charles Leiserson, Ronald Rivest and Clifford Stein, Introduction to Algorithms (4th ed., MIT Press, 2022): the standard comprehensive reference, recommended for advanced topics and for full proofs.

Free online books:

  • Jeff Erickson, Algorithms (University of Illinois): recursion, dynamic programming, greedy algorithms and their correctness, graph algorithms, lower bounds.
  • Pat Morin, Open Data Structures: the core data structures, with a C++ edition.
  • Charles A. Cusack and David A. Santos, An Active Introduction to Discrete Mathematics and Algorithms (2018, PDF): proofs, induction, recursion, counting, algorithm analysis, graphs.
  • William Chen, Discrete Mathematics (lecture notes, Macquarie University): logic, induction, counting, recurrences, graphs.

How the course runs

Lectures

Two 2-hour lectures a week, interactive: predict, run, explain. The slides, the course text and the demos are on the course hub. There are no recordings.

Weekly quizzes

20 minutes on paper, multiple choice, on Mondays, on the previous week's lectures and homework. Eight quizzes of 35 points each (3.5 points a question); the best six count. There are no make-up quizzes: the two dropped scores cover absences.

Homework

One homework a week in C++: a starter file with the parts to write and a test program that runs itself, practice for the quiz that follows. Due Sunday at 11:59 PM; graded by an autograder, 10 points each; the best eight of ten count.

Submitting: homework and programming assignments are submitted on Canvas as one .zip file holding your source files and anything else the assignment asks for (a report, images, a screenshot of your tests). You can resubmit until the deadline; the last submission is graded.

Programming assignments

Three larger builds of 70 points each, one per block of the course: search trees, graphs, and dynamic programming. Individual work. Graded on the CSS Linux lab with g++ -std=c++17: code that does not compile there receives no points, and from the first assignment on, memory leaks cost points (see the Valgrind guide). The paper quiz after each due date asks about your assignment.

Exams

Multiple choice, on paper. Midterm (200 points) Mon Nov 9, in class, on everything through shortest paths; comprehensive final (300 points) Mon Dec 14, 1:15–3:15 PM. No make-up exams, earlier or later.

Policies

Late work and late tokens

Everyone has 2 late tokens for the term. A token extends one homework or programming assignment deadline by 48 hours; it does not apply to quizzes or exams. Without a token, late work is not accepted, and nothing is accepted after the last day of instruction, Fri Dec 11. Submit on Canvas, not by email.

Academic integrity

Programming assignments and homework are individual work; copied code is misconduct. If misconduct is suspected: (1) the instructor contacts you to discuss it; (2) refusing to engage is reported to the school with documentation; (3) if you are responsible and say so before a referral, you receive zero on the work and are not reported; (4) if you are responsible and do not, the case goes to the school, which can mean failing the course with a recorded violation.

Generative AI

AI tools may be used for learning, and for limited help on assignments if you disclose it: cite the tool, summarize your prompts, and note any AI-assisted code in a comment. Using AI for a whole assignment is misconduct, and AI is not allowed during quizzes or exams. If you used none, say so: “I did not use any AI tools for this assignment.”

Attendance

There are no recordings and no make-up lectures. You are responsible for all material covered in class.

Disability accommodations

Contact Disability Resources for Students early, within the first two weeks, and have your DRS counselor contact the instructor. Exam accommodations must be requested through DRS at least 10 business days before each exam.

Grade questions

Bring any grading concern to the instructor within one week of the work being returned; after that, grades are final.
